# Managing SE Projects: the agency perspective

## Core Idea

An agency that will own the operational system acquires development services, in-house or contracted, and remains responsible for the result. Success starts with role clarity (owner, operator, maintainer, installer, developer, certifier) and with an honest risk evaluation, because each project's inherent risk level determines how much systems engineering process to apply. The chapter's method: evaluate risk, tailor the process to that risk, choose procurement and staffing approaches that fit agency capability, and implement the federal requirements through regional architecture work and a systems engineering analysis sized to the project.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Seven risk factors, each graded low, medium, or high: number of jurisdictions and modes; extent of software creation; maturity of hardware and communications technology; number and complexity of new interfaces; completeness of written requirements; completeness of written operating procedures; and remaining service life of the technologies involved. Staff experience and clarity of user needs modify the picture.
- A three-band tailoring table across the Vee steps: low-risk projects reuse existing documentation at none-to-low effort; medium-risk projects update and extend; high-risk projects develop the full documentation set, with feasibility study, requirements and verification plan, and design definition at medium effort and implementation at medium-high.
- Procurement instruments: RFI for market input (cannot lead directly to award), RFQ with qualifications-based selection under the Brooks Act tradition, IDIQ task-order contracting, RFP with best-value or lowest-responsive-bid evaluation, ITB for well-specified products, and public-private partnerships.
- Implementation models: design-bid-build, design-build, and construction manager at risk with a guaranteed maximum price; staffing options of self-performance, contracted SE performance, and contracted SE monitoring, chosen per phase.

## Key Concepts

Risk evaluation is not binary: if the seven factors mix levels, the agency customizes attention around the high factors, for example concentrating on requirements when nothing else is risky. Examples calibrate the bands: adding thirty identical signs to a healthy system is low risk; adding shared control with a partner agency turns the same project high risk through new software, new interfaces, and new agreements. First stages of umbrella systems are high risk; later replications of the initial implementation fall back to low risk.

Tailoring is the deliberate matching of formality to risk: the same step can be an engineer's notebook entry on one project and a configuration-controlled product on another, and the process must be written down (as a project plan or SEMP) so team and stakeholders agree on the steps and depth. Procurement choices carry consequences: most SE service contracts use qualifications-based selection; folding SE into a cost-competing design-build contract invites underfunded or understaffed engineering. Self-performance gives direct control but strains staffing, discipline, and objective evaluation, since in-house evaluators have both the appearance of bias and the incentive to report success.

Implementation of 23 CFR 940 runs through the regional ITS architecture, built from the ARC-IT national reference with the RAD-IT tool, and through the seven analysis elements demonstrated per project. Many states use a Systems Engineering Review Form or checklist to collect the seven demonstrations; the source walks through what to collect for each, from architecture mapping (service packages, elements, information flows) through requirements, alternatives, procurement options, standards (an MPO can generate a standards report from the architecture's information flows), and operations roles and resources.

## Mental Models

- The agency owns the outcome even when it outsources every activity: it must always participate in and approve findings, schedules, budgets, agreements, and the SE rigor chosen.
- Risk evaluation as a spectrum, not a gate: the seven factors steer where the engineering attention concentrates.
- Documentation as the ownership handoff: written requirements and procedures are what make an upgrade or a second operator possible.

## Anti-patterns

Tailoring is not an invitation to skip steps: even small projects need documented requirements, design, and verification procedures. Errors in the procurement process invite bid protests and lawsuits that stall projects. In-house self-performance under schedule or political pressure can silently curtail SE processes; the countermeasure named is a visible entry in the risk register. Design-build incentives to underfund SE are flagged directly.

## Key Takeaways

- Evaluate risk first; tailor second; write the tailored process down third.
- The seven risk factors double as the checklist for what makes an ITS project risky, and software creation is the steepest driver.
- Match the contract instrument to what is actually known: qualifications for early or open scope, best-value RFP for defined requirements, ITB for purchasable products.
- Compliance is demonstrated project by project, commonly via a review form mapping to the seven analysis elements.
