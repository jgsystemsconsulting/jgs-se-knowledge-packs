# Glossary and Acronyms

## Core Idea

The source's final section defines the vocabulary the document uses and a wider set of terms common in systems engineering, plus an acronym list covering roughly ninety entries. Its own framing: while many definitions can be used, each project will have its own set of terms to define and adopt as part of initial tasks. The glossary is therefore both a reference and a template for project-level terminology work; the pack's own glossary.md draws on it, and this chapter records how the source organizes vocabulary.

## Frameworks Introduced

- A single alphabetical glossary spanning lifecycle terms (life cycle, life cycle model, control gates), product structure terms (assembly, component, configuration item, end products, enabling products, module, part), process terms (elicitation, decomposition, synthesis, gap analysis, needs assessment), and management terms (baseline, configuration management, risk management, tailoring).
- An acronym list that doubles as a map of the field's institutions and artifacts: agencies and societies (AASHTO, FHWA, FTA, IEEE, INCOSE, ITE, SAE, NEMA), review gates (SRR, PDR, CDR, SDR, TRR), document types (ICD, SOW, SEMP, SERF), and communications standards bodies behind NTCIP.

## Key Concepts

Definitions that carry the pack's weight: system as an integrated composite of people, products, and processes providing capability to satisfy a stated need; system of systems as a system whose elements are themselves systems, with the regional transportation system given as the example; verification as determining that the products of a phase fulfill the requirements of the preceding phase, and validation as determining that the system meets the user's needs. Tailoring is defined as planning systems engineering activities appropriate and cost-effective for the size and complexity of the project, driven by cost, size, stakeholders, interfaces, ownership (custom software versus commercial off-the-shelf), resources, and risks.

The acquisition vocabulary (acquirer, contractor, subcontractor, supplier, vendor, contract, acceptance, approval, authentication) mirrors procurement practice, and the distinction between acceptance (assuming ownership) and approval (written notification that plans look sound) is kept precise. ITS-specific definitions tie the general terms to the domain: intelligent transportation systems as the broad range of information processing, communications, control, and electronics technologies improving surface transportation; regional ITS architecture as the regional framework for institutional agreement and technical integration; operational concept as the roles and responsibilities of primary stakeholders and the systems they operate; and service packages as the service-oriented building blocks of the national architecture, implemented through projects and tied to regional strategies.

## Mental Models

- Definitions are project artifacts: adopt a set explicitly at project start rather than inheriting ambiguity.
- Terms come in contrasting pairs the discipline depends on: verification versus validation, acceptance versus approval, end products versus enabling products, functional versus physical decomposition.

## Anti-patterns

None stated. The section is definitional; its only caution is the general one that projects must actively choose and adopt their terms.

## Key Takeaways

- Every specialized term used in ch01 through ch06 resolves here or in the pack glossary.
- The acronym list is the fastest decoder for review meetings: gates, documents, and standards bodies all abbreviate.
- Project terminology adoption is itself a task: the source treats the glossary as a starting inventory, not a finished vocabulary.
