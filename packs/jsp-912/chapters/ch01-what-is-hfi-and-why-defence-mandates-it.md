# What HFI is and why Defence mandates it (Parts 1 and 2)

## Core Idea

Human Factors Integration (HFI) is a management activity, not a branch of engineering. Part 1 defines it as a systematic process for identifying, tracking and resolving people-related considerations, especially risks and issues, so that the technological and human aspects of capability develop in balance. The publication is careful to separate three often-confused terms: Human Factors (HF) is the scientific study of human capabilities, limitations, characteristics and behaviour, drawing on ergonomics, psychology, physiology, biology, anthropometry and biomechanics; Human Factors Engineering (HFE) is the application of that knowledge across design, development, assessment, fielding, in-service support and disposal; and HFI is the management process that sits over both. Part 1 states plainly that HFI is more than HFE.

The mandate is unconditional in coverage: MOD policy applies HFI to every defence acquisition activity, with Development Items, Non-Development Items, Off-The-Shelf purchases, technology demonstrators and upgrades to existing items all named as in scope. In every system providing Defence Capability, the Equipment Component and the Human Component shall be satisfactorily integrated, so that the roles given to people enable the capability performance required under all predicted conditions of use.

Part 2 adds the composition view: HFI combines People, Processes and Technology into an integrated system capable of safe, effective and efficient performance across the full range of anticipated operating conditions. The People component covers military personnel and civilian support staff who operate, maintain and support the capability, together with the organisations they work in; the Technology component covers all equipment, hardware, software and information; organisational and management Processes link the two, and all of it must be considered against the Environment of use.

## Frameworks Introduced

- The five HFI domains, named in Part 1 and elaborated in Part 2: Personnel, Training, Human Factors Engineering, System Safety & Health Hazards, and Organisational & Social. Personnel concerns numbers and characteristics of people needed and available; Training covers knowledge and skills development to a specified effectiveness; HFE integrates human physical and cognitive characteristics into system definition and design; System Safety & Health Hazards concerns operating without death, injury or illness, including health hazards from noise, vibration, fumes or unhygienic conditions; Organisational & Social applies organisational psychology and social science to configuration and ways of working.
- Domain ownership is deliberately dispersed. Part 1 maps the domains against stakeholders and disciplines (Training DLOD owners, project managers, human factors engineers, systems engineers, safety engineers, training specialists, chief engineers, solution provider engineers) and concludes that the domains are not entirely owned by the Human Factors Specialist; different related disciplines must interact to deliver HFI.
- The relative weight of domains shifts with procurement type and risk: Part 2 notes that a commercial off-the-shelf capability introduced into a new organisation raises more Training and Organisational & Social concerns than a Development Item, where HFE may dominate. An Early Human Factors Analysis establishes which domains matter most on a given project.

## Key Concepts

Part 1 sets integration duties on the design and realisation of the Solution: make best use of human capabilities (physical, psychological and social characteristics), recognise and provide for human needs, provide mitigations for human limitations, apply to all people involved in fielding the system (End Users, an all-encompassing term covering operators, maintainers, trainers and support personnel regardless of Service, rank or role), utilise people in ways that maximise system safety, utilise people cost-effectively, and control through-life costs, for example by minimising training burden and the personnel needed to operate and maintain the system.

Part 2 itemises the cost of neglect: more accidents and incidents, costlier training, weaker performance and mission effectiveness, duty-of-care breaches, shortages of appropriately skilled personnel, project delays, and substantial design or redesign bills. Even uninhabited systems still need people to operate, maintain and support them.

Scope is risk-proportionate: the scope, extent, depth, complexity and thoroughness of HFI activities shall be determined against the risk that people-related considerations present to project and programme outcomes, assessed in terms of capability goals, cost, time, system performance, system safety and system usability. The policy applies from the outset of capability development, where early decisions on requirements, concepts of use, design and assumptions determine ultimate effectiveness, and throughout the capability's life. It binds all MOD staff across the life cycle and names the stakeholders it especially addresses: customers (capability planners, sponsors, requirements managers), Delivery Agents, Defence Line of Development owners, trials units, Specialist Engineering Functions, and End Users.

Solution Provider HFI activities shall be contracted against Defence Standard 00-251, the primary standard for HFI and HFE. Other named guidance includes Defence Standard 23-009 on Generic Vehicle Architecture, the System Readiness Levels, the Guide to Engineering Activities and Reviews, and Defence Standard 00-600 on Integrated Logistics Support. These are named here, not reproduced.

## Mental Models

- Domains are coupled, not silos: Part 2 warns that a decision in one domain easily affects another; raising the level of automation, for example, can change required staffing levels, and vice versa.
- A cheap prompt travels far: at the simplest level, asking whether there are concerns relating to each domain helps identify risks to the system, project or programme, and belongs inside the project's risk management activity.
- HFE contributes to the people-related issues of the other domains, which are normally led by their respective discipline specialists; the human factors specialist does not own all human outcomes.

## Anti-patterns

Part 1 refuses to prescribe a single set of detailed HFI activities. The publication prescribes high-level activities applicable to all acquisition projects precisely because the range and diversity of projects make one detailed prescription impossible; the detailed process lives in Part 2, tailored per project. Treating the five-domain framework as a human factors specialist's private checklist is the failure mode the domain-ownership table exists to prevent.

## Key Takeaways

- HFI is a mandated management process; HFE is one of its five domains, not a synonym for it.
- Coverage is total across acquisition types; proportionality enters through risk-based scoping, not through exemption.
- The Human Component of Capability is a first-class acquisition object with named integration duties on the Solution.
- Five domains, many owners: integration across disciplines is the point, and domain coupling means early decisions propagate.
