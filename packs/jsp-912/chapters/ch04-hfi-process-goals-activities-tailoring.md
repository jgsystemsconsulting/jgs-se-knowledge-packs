# HFI process goals, activities and tailoring (Parts 1 and 2)

## Core Idea

Part 1 section 2 fixes seven HFI process goals that shall be fully pursued in every MOD capability acquisition project, and requires every HFI activity undertaken to relate to and support one or more of them. Part 2 repeats the goals and adds the test: when each goal has been satisfactorily addressed, a project may claim that HFI has been satisfactorily achieved. The same sections prescribe the six top-level process stages, which span the full CADMID span from Pre-Concept to Disposal, and set the tailoring regime that keeps the process proportionate.

The six stages are named in Part 2 as: User Need Definition, System Requirements Definition, Assess Tenders, Detailed System Design, Test and Acceptance, and In-Service Feedback. They are applicable to all types of Capability Acquisition project on a generic lifecycle model including CADMID and ISO/IEC 15288-style lifecycles, though Part 2 concedes an exact alignment with CADMID is not possible because project categories differ slightly. HFI in the Disposal phase is not a unique process: its contribution is normally treated within detailed design activities, or run through stages 1 to 6 on a much smaller scale.

## Frameworks Introduced

- The seven goals: identify and manage all people-related Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies and Opportunities (RAIDO) from the very outset and through life; ensure Human Factors Process Requirements (HFPRs) are specified so HFI processes are properly undertaken; ensure Human Factors System Requirements (HFSRs) are specified, with Part 2 adding Human Factors User Requirements (HFURs), so the people-related technical aspects are properly addressed against the RAIDO; adopt a human-centred design approach involving End Users in design and evaluation; use established HF principles, accepted best practice and suitable methods, tools, techniques and data; design the HFI programme to align and integrate with the project life cycle; and subject people-related considerations of the Solution to formal scrutiny, assessment and acceptance.
- Tailoring is fundamental: Part 1 defines tailoring as deciding, and spelling out, how broad and how deep the set of HFI activities should be, given the project's scope, size, complexity, life cycle phase and contractual arrangements. Before Outline Business Case the Authority shall consider internally the range and depth of HFI it expects, including a pan-DLOD view of the areas of greatest perceived HFI risk identified by an Early Human Factors Analysis. The process shall be revisited during tender preparation so tailoring assumptions remain valid, and the results are reflected in documentation issued to tenderers. Further tailoring may follow Contract Award, conducted jointly by the Solution Provider and the Project or Delivery Team with key stakeholders' agreement, with the final decision on acceptability made by MOD.
- Project typing drives effort: equipment, in-service support, service provision, ICT, and infrastructure are the five main MOD investment project types. For equipment development there are three introduction routes: Development Item, for which the HFI process should be followed in full with per-activity tailoring; Non-Development Item, where research and design are complete and a reduced process applies, since no design activity is needed and test and acceptance shrink; and Off-The-Shelf, a subset of NDI built to commercial standards with minimal MOD design influence, including Modified or Military off the Shelf variants.
- Assurance hooks: through the lifecycle, HFI maturity is assured from the engineering perspective using the Guide to Engineering Activities and Reviews and the System Readiness Levels, and from the support perspective using the Support Solutions Envelope's Support Solutions Development Tool.

## Key Concepts

The business-case rhythm frames the stages: Outline Business Case is where the Capability Sponsor and Delivery Team seek approval of the Concept of Analysis; Full Business Case is the major decision point at which the Solution, supported by mature URD and SRD specifications, is approved. Some HFI activities can only be carried out once the project has reached a certain development stage, so timing and duration must be synchronised with other project activities to keep information flowing between stakeholders and between Acquirer and Solution Provider. MOD staff or contracted representatives shall organise and conduct tailored HFI activities systematically, commensurate with the project's phase, size and complexity; Part 2 explains that the scope, extent, depth and thoroughness of all HFI activities should be determined by consideration of the risks to the required project outcomes, measured in capability goals, objectives, cost, time, system performance, system safety and system usability.

For OTS equipment, design data on which to base HFI test and acceptance may not exist commercially and may need to be calculated, predicted or measured on delivered products; although the process may not influence the design, it should still be used to identify risks and concerns, identify mitigations for introduction, identify any modifications needed for military use, and establish what human factors analyses were applied during commercial design. OTS products differ from OTS-based systems: an OTS product is a single item or pre-integrated configuration sold as a system, while an OTS-based system is MOD's own configuration of many OTS components, closer to a development item in HFI effort at the detailed design stage. Where NDI or COTS items reduce design work, additional focus may fall on the non-equipment DLODs such as personnel and training, because the human component must adapt to the item.

## Mental Models

- Goals as a completeness test: any activity that supports none of the seven goals is questionable; any goal with no activities is a gap.
- Tailoring by extremes: the DI and OTS poles bracket the decision space, and most modern procurements, being mixtures, land between them with cost and complexity tipping the balance.
- Staged contracting fits uncertainty: sequential contracts or a long contract with break points let maturity evidence gate commitment.

## Anti-patterns

Part 1 refuses to prescribe a single set of detailed HFI activities for all projects; the detailed process is tailored, not copied. Part 2 adds a structural guard: although every stage may be tailored and the detail of each core activity is tailorable, the majority of core activities are mandatory and very few can be tailored out completely. Treating tailoring as deletion of inconvenient stages, rather than adjustment of range and depth, contradicts the source.

## Key Takeaways

- Seven goals, six stages, one rule: every activity supports at least one goal, and satisfaction of the goals is the claim of HFI achievement.
- Tailoring starts internally before OBC, is re-tested at tender preparation, and is revisited jointly after contract award, with MOD holding the final decision.
- Project type sets the default shape: DI full process, NDI reduced, OTS risk-focused on introduction; OTS-based systems behave like development items.
