# Chapter 2: Roles and safety in the programme

## Safety inside capability management

Through-life capability management depends on a capability being planned, delivered, and maintained so that it is safe to operate and is operated safely. Operation covers use, maintenance, repair, storage, transportation, and disposal. Heads of Defence organisations, the Defence Nuclear Organisation and Strategic Programmes among them, must make sure that Capability Sponsors weigh safety explicitly in the Develop function, beside the other factors, in Capability Management Strategies, in the Capability Management Plans sitting under them, and in governance forums such as Capability Management Groups.

The foreword places this policy in its setting: JSP 376 builds on JSP 815, codifies acquisition safety requirements and safety artefact expectations, and embeds safety assessment in the Defence Investment Approvals process set by JSP 655. That is a fact about JSP 376's position, not an invitation to restate either publication, and this pack does not.

## The programme delivery envelope

The programme delivery envelope, across all DLODs, has three sides: Performance (also called Quality), Cost, and Time. A change in one side can force a change in the others. Safety is a key element of Performance and is considered beside the other performance requirements, which means a change to cost, to time, or to another performance parameter can move safety. The SRO must make sure safety implications across all DLODs are considered from the outset, because early design choices are where hazards are cheapest to remove.

## The Defence Lines of Development

The envelope spans the eight Defence Lines of Development (DLODs): Training, Equipment, Personnel, Information, Concepts and Doctrine, Organisation, Infrastructure, and Logistics, remembered as TEPIDOIL, with Interoperability as the overarching theme.

## Interfaces

Internally, the SRO/User appoints SQEP individuals to deliver elements of the programme and keeps oversight of those elements, with special attention at the boundaries between them, where hazards and responsibilities meet. Externally, safety attention extends to the boundary with other programmes, including ones already in service, because a new or changed capability rarely stands alone.

## Competence

People are matched to roles through competence. The source's terms are SQEP for the suitably qualified and experienced person a role needs, and SKEB for safety skills, knowledge, experience, and behaviours. Many policies in this space require a competent person. JSP 815 Volume 2 Element 6 is the competence direction; it is named here and not restated.

## Safety leadership

Safety leadership is a personal responsibility of SROs and Users, and the foreword says so directly. It has three parts. The first is a strong safety culture: shared values, attitudes, and behaviours, with JSP 815 Element 1 named as the direction. The second is collaboration across the sponsor, delivery agents, operators, industry, regulators, and approving authorities, because no one of them holds the whole safety picture. The third is psychological safety: an environment where staff can raise a safety concern without fear of negative repercussions. Alongside the three, the SRO/User makes sure safety is considered at all times, beside the other aspects of the programme.

## Lifecycle shape

Acquisition runs through seven phases: Pre-Concept plus CADMID/T, which is Concept, Assessment, Demonstration, Manufacture, In-Service, and Disposal/Termination. Pre-Concept lines up with the Treasury Green Book. Each phase executes the plan agreed in the previous phase, reviews the outcome, and plans what remains. Formal approving-authority decisions fall at the end of the first three phases, each on a business case: the Strategic Outline Case at the end of Pre-Concept, the Outline Business Case at the end of Concept, and the Full Business Case at the end of Assessment. The greatest opportunity to influence safety is early, and late mitigation costs more in performance, cost, and time. Where phases run concurrently, complexity, technical risk, and safety risk all increase. The SRO/User applies the lifecycle flexibly and demonstrates that application in the business case. Annex B of the source shows how safety case and safety management system ownership moves across the phases.

## Acquisition reform

JSP 376 supports five acquisition reform themes. Cost estimating and cost control: the cost of safety activity is estimated and controlled from early on and through life. Relationships with industry: oversight and transparency of safety risk. Linking requirements to strategic intent: safety is considered beside other strategic factors from the outset. Empowering programme leadership: leaders get a clear picture of requirements and expectations, with safety inside the performance parameters. Systems, accountabilities, and processes: accountabilities stay clear where elements are assigned to a delivery agent or contracted out.

## The Chapter 4 gap

Version 1.1 of the source marks its Chapter 4, Non-Standard Acquisition, as `[to follow]`, gives no page, and prints no body for it. The gap is recorded here as a gap; this pack does not invent the missing chapter. What Chapter 1 of the source does give is a set of pointers, and each stays a pointer rather than a procedure. For agile and adaptive acquisition, SRO responsibilities are unchanged, and every output delivered for use, interim or final, needs to comply with safety requirements and be supported by a safety case, or the SRO engages early for an exemption, waiver, or concession agreed before use. For innovation and experimentation, safety accountabilities are agreed and documented, activity is risk-assessed before it happens, and one output is the assessment of how the capability will meet later safety requirements and certification. For programmable elements, software, firmware, and data faults can lie dormant until a revealing mechanism finds them, so the SRO/User follows JSP 935 and makes sure safe-software techniques are implemented; Defence Standard 00-055, Requirements for Safety of Programmable Elements in Defence Systems, is named by title only. For cyber security, a system cannot be trusted to be safe if it is not secure, safety and security objectives do not always align, and the direction is dialogue throughout with Secure by Design, under JSP 440. For autonomous systems and AI, the potential to remove people from danger comes with new uncertainties where systems decide without human control, so safety assurance is actively considered, under JSP 936.

## Responsibilities

**SRO.** Usually sits in a Military Command, the Defence Nuclear Organisation, or Head Office Strategic Programmes. Accountable for a capability that is safe to operate and for the work of delivery being conducted safely: setting safety requirements, contracting for safety, assuring against legislation, regulation, and standards, mitigating through the hierarchy of controls, and seeing that residual risk is declared ALARP and tolerable and communicated to the User.

**User.** Usually sits in a Military Command. Accountable for the capability being maintained safe to operate and operated safely: suitable and sufficient risk assessment inside a safe system of work, residual risk communicated to operators, operation kept inside the defined method of use, envelope, and configuration and maintained to procedures, information, instruction, training, and supervision provided, and incidents reported and investigated. Where enhanced arrangements are required, the User may also be a Duty Holder under JSP 815 Element 5.

**T&E User.** Accountable for safe operation where MOD controls the test and evaluation activity, carrying the same accountabilities for that activity as the User carries in service. May be the User, come from the User's organisation, or come from another.

**Lead User.** Where there is more than one User, one is appointed accountable for the User responsibilities in consultation with all User organisations. Where the policy says "User", it includes a Lead User.

**Approving Authority and Requirements Oversight Committees.** The Approving Authority is the person or committee with authority to approve an investment decision, per JSP 655. Requirements Oversight Committees, including the Joint ROC and organisation-specific ROCs, confirm the requirement is understood, valid, aligned, and deliverable across all DLODs, and from a safety perspective they help shape High-Level Characteristics and Key User Requirements so that safe intent is captured.

**Approvals Decision Support Community.** Provides expert independent analysis and advice to the approving authority, per JSP 655.

**Delivery Agents.** Deliver elements, including through industry contracts, and produce much of the safety evidence. Four enabling organisations are set up for the role: DE&S, SDA, Defence Digital, and DIO, and the SRO/User may use others or their own team. In every case, the delivery agent's safety responsibilities are written into the safety management plan and any supporting assignment such as a Command Acquisition Support Plan, and accountability stays with the SRO/User.

**DSA.** Independent, per the DSA Charter. Leads health, safety, and environmental protection regulation, assures the Secretary of State through the Permanent Secretary, and sets the domain-specific regulatory requirements that programmes follow and against which DSA assures.

**Dir DS.** Holds Safety Functional Leadership for the Chief Operating Officer, owns JSP 815, and runs corporate governance of Defence safety for the Permanent Secretary. Permanent safety advisor to the IAC, the JROC, and the Defence Major Projects Portfolio Sponsor Group, supported by the ASC.

**ASC.** Part of DDS. Provides approvals decision support for acquisition safety, primarily for high risk and complex programmes, and sits as a functional member of the Head Office Approvals Decision Support Community. Its analysis and advice say whether a programme is managed in accordance with JSP 376, drawn from existing artefacts; it does not express an opinion on the safety of the programme's outputs or outcomes.
