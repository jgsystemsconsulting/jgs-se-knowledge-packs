# Chapter 1: Acquisition safety policy

## Purpose

JSP 376 standardises and simplifies acquisition safety so that the SRO/User delivers capabilities that are safe to operate and, once in service, are operated safely. The publication does four things. It provides a standardised programme-level approach, so safety is considered at programme level from the outset. It states accountabilities with clarity, amplifying JSP 815 rather than repeating it. It gives direction on treating safety as an enabler that sits beside other performance criteria. And it sets evidence expectations that are consistent with JSP 655 and the approvals decision support obligations. JSP 815 and JSP 655 are named here as the surrounding framework; this pack does not restate what either contains.

## The six concepts

Chapter 1 of the source frames acquisition safety with six concepts, named only: Safety Management; Safety Requirements; Test and Evaluation (including Certification); Safety Case; Supervision and Control Activities; and Acquisition Safety Assurance and Approvals Decision Support. The source's Chapter 2 turns each concept into policy, and its Chapter 3 then places the concepts on the lifecycle phases. In this pack, ch03 carries safety management and safety requirements, ch04 carries test and evaluation, certification, and the safety case, and ch05 carries supervision and control, assurance, and approvals decision support.

## Proportionality

Proportionality is fundamental to how the policy is meant to be used. JSP 376 sets goals and describes what good looks like instead of prescribing a single method. That choice is what lets SROs and Users tailor their safety approach to a programme's size, complexity, and safety risk, rather than applying one shape of process to everything.

## Scope

The policy applies to all acquisition programmes in all Defence organisations.

## Terms the reader needs

### Acquisition safety

Acquisition safety is the application of management principles and techniques, across procurement, operation, and disposal, to deliver a capability that is safe to operate and can be operated safely.

### The SRO

The SRO is the individual accountable for delivery of the programme. Early in the lifecycle, the seat may be filled by a Capability Sponsor or a Transformation Manager rather than a conventional SRO.

### The SRO/User

Much of the policy is addressed to "the SRO/User", because which of the two is accountable usually depends on the lifecycle stage: the SRO up to entry to service, the User after it. Where it is unclear which applies, the two consult each other, and the DDS ASC advises.

### Programme, project, element

The programme is the pan-DLOD construct within which the change outcome is delivered, and it is what the SRO is accountable for. A project or element is a part of that construct that the SRO may assign to others; accountability for the assigned part stays with the SRO.

### Head Office and delegated approvals

The policy is written for Head Office approvals: Dir DS is the safety advisor to the IAC, supported by the ASC, and references to the IAC include the IAC(Nuclear). Defence organisations put the same measures in place for delegated approving authorities, and the terms AA and ASC cover both cases.

## Legislative baseline

UK health and safety legislation requires risks to be assessed and reduced to as low as reasonably practicable (ALARP), with secondary legislation setting prescribed limits for some hazards. The Health and Safety at Work etc Act 1974 is the anchor. Recognised good practice is where an ALARP demonstration usually starts, in the HSE's sense of the measures normally expected in similar circumstances.

Where benefits do not clearly outweigh costs, a cost-benefit comparison is needed, and that comparison must favour safety. Controls are implemented unless the sacrifice, which includes time and operational implications and not only money, is grossly disproportionate to the risk reduction. JSP 507 gives the appraisal direction, and Defence Economics should be contacted before any ALARP cost-benefit analysis.

An exemption is a last resort, taken after other courses of action have been discounted. Before entry to service the SRO consults the User, justifies the case, and sponsors the exemption under JSP 815 Volume 2 Annex B; after entry to service the User sponsors it.

Tolerability is wider than ALARP. A risk can be reduced as far as practicable and still be intolerable to wider society, so the SRO/User considers both questions and not only the first.

Controls are applied in a hierarchy, from most to least effective: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment. The source attributes that list to JSP 375 Volume 1 Chapter 8. The early phases are where controls are most embeddable, which is why the hierarchy points design activity at elimination and substitution first.

## Policy framework

JSP 376 is Defence-level, pan-domain policy that draws JSP 815 and acquisition policy together. DDS owns Defence safety policy. DSA, an independent enabling organisation, develops and manages Defence safety regulations and gives domain-level direction on implementing JSP 376. Heads of Defence organisations then direct implementation inside their own organisations. For contracting with industry, Defence Standard 00-056 sets requirements and guidance for the achievement, assurance, and management of safety. The standard is named here and not quoted.

## Preface terminology

JSP 376 is published as a single part: directive content and guidance are combined rather than split into volumes. The preface fixes the meaning of three operative terms, and the rest of this pack depends on that wording. The preface definition, quoted from paragraph 2 of printed page ii:

> Where this policy says must, this means that the action is a compulsory requirement to be completed by the actioner. Where the term 'must make sure' is used, the action may be delivered by an assigned individual, but the actioner remains accountable for these actions being conducted. Where this policy says should, this means that the action is not a compulsory requirement but is considered recommended good practice.

In short: `must` binds the named actioner, `must make sure` allows the work to be delivered by someone else while accountability stays with the actioner, and `should` marks recommended good practice. This pack uses the three words the same way.
