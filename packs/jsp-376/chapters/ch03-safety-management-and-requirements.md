# Chapter 3: Safety management and safety requirements

## The safety management key policy statement

The source states the safety management policy in one sentence, quoted here from the Safety Management key policy statement:

> The SRO/User must make sure that a Safety Management Plan (SMP) is put in place to describe how they intend a capability to be safely delivered, operated, and disposed of, in accordance with the parent organisations Safety Management System (SMS).

## Plan-Do-Check-Act

The HSE, in HSG65, and JSP 815 recommend Plan-Do-Check-Act as a structure, and it applies to a change programme as much as to routine business.

**Plan.** Safety is included in the programme management plan, and key safety tasks sit in the integrated master schedule. Each programme has an SMP, inside the parent SMS arrangements, covering all elements and DLODs and proportionate to the programme's size, complexity, and risk. A programme SMP differs from a delivery-team SMP, which may cover a single DLOD element. Typical contents: a description of the capability; the key safety requirements; deliverables and milestones, such as the safety case report and, where required, certification; structures, roles, responsibilities, and competence requirements, supported by letters of appointment or delegation and terms of reference; governance, including safety inside management of change; the safety risk management process, including escalation; and the assurance process, including safety performance indicators.

**Do.** Implement the plan. Safety risk management identifies and addresses key safety risks inside overall delivery, and those key safety risks go into the programme risk register with real weighting, not only into a separate safety register. Strong governance holds those with safety accountabilities to their outputs.

**Check.** Monitor the safety elements as part of programme governance. Accidents, incidents, and near misses are investigated so that lessons are identified, including during test and evaluation, with JSP 375 Volume 1 Chapter 16 named as the direction.

**Act.** Actions from monitoring, from assurance including regulatory enforcement, and from investigations are implemented, so the capability is delivered safe and its ALARP and tolerable status holds once delivered.

## Organisational Safety Assessment

The Organisational Safety Assessment (OSA) is a JSP 375 product: JSP 375 Volume 1 Chapter 35 directs it, and it identifies the safety risks of a proposed change and the controls needed so there is no adverse impact on personnel or on the safe conduct of Defence activities. Organisational change here is wider than structure; it includes pan-DLOD changes to duties, and a significant new capability solution is likely to be in scope. The SRO must formally consider whether an OSA is needed during procurement and be able to justify a decision that one is not. The User does the same for in-service changes, including a change in how the capability is used. The safety case is important evidence inside that assessment; it is not a substitute for the decision to do one. Where an OSA is done, the SRO/User should include a clear declaration of no reasonably foreseeable detriment to safety.

## Safety responsibilities

SRO and User engage early and throughout. The SRO seeks the in-service perspective, and the User provides it; where the User is not yet appointed, the SRO arranges in-service input from someone who may not become the User. Authorities, accountabilities, and responsibilities are documented from the SRO appointment letter down, through the SMP, letters, and terms of reference, with key responsibilities acknowledged in writing. Where industry carries safety responsibilities, they are written into the contract. Staff are competent for their roles. Where there is more than one User, Heads of Defence organisations agree and appoint a Lead User.

## Digital engineering, quality, and information

Digital tools such as digital twins, live data feeds, and digital safety cases should be actively considered, agreed early with the relevant authorities such as certification bodies, and still produce an evidence-based argument. Quality and safety are linked: JSP 940 requires proportionate processes that assure the quality of products, services, and systems, with attention to safety-critical ones. Safety information has to be managed through life, or the safety case is undermined: evidence must be accessible when required, and residual-risk information must stay current for the people who need it. The SMP defines what information sustains the safety case and how it is managed, in accordance with JSP 441, JSP 375 Volume 1 Chapter 39, and relevant Defence safety regulations.

## The safety requirements key policy statement

The source states the safety requirements policy in one sentence, quoted here from the Safety Requirements key policy statement:

> The SRO must make sure that the User Requirements Document (URD) captures all safety User Requirements (URs) and reflects the need for the capability to be both 'safe to operate' and 'operated safely' for a given application in a given operating environment.

## How safety requirements flow

Requirements stem from capability requirements set in the operating context of the CONEMP and then the CONUSE, developed early under Concepts and Doctrine. From those come the Single Statement of User Need and the High-Level Characteristics, and then the URD. Safety user requirements sit under Performance in the URD. They should be SMART, developed through early stakeholder consultation with the User and with interdependent capabilities, with human factors input under JSP 912. An audit trail records the time, date, status changes, and rationale for each change.

## The safety KUR

For programmes of high risk and complexity, the SRO must include a candidate safety Key User Requirement. For lower-risk programmes, the SRO must formally consider one, and a decision not to have one must be justifiable to the Requirements Oversight Committee and the approving authority at SOC and OBC. The need and the structure of the KUR are discussed with the ASC.

## Through-life requirements

Disposal and termination requirements are considered early, so the design does not acquire attributes that are hard to mitigate later, such as hazardous materials, or stored energy that cannot later be recovered, disarmed, or made safe. Requirements are reviewed through life.

## Measures of effectiveness

Measures of effectiveness give objective evidence that a safety user requirement is met. "ALARP and tolerable" is not used as such a measure: that judgement belongs to the User, taken from the safety case argument. The measures themselves are agreed with the ASC.

## Prioritisation and trades

Safety user requirements are prioritised beside the other user requirements, including in options selection and in trades. User requirements can be traded; the safety risk must still end up ALARP and tolerable, even where a capability trade leaves the risk higher than it was. Legal and regulatory requirements cannot be traded out unless an exemption is available and has been granted. Legislation carries absolute, prescriptive, and proscriptive requirements as well as ALARP ones.

## Legal and regulatory requirements

Statutory and regulatory requirements, including Defence safety regulations, are identified, assessed, recorded, and monitored through life. The Defence Legislation Support Tool assists with that.

## DEDs and exemptions

Where legislation contains a disapplication, exemption, or derogation for Defence, the associated Defence safety regulation is followed. An exemption, waiver, or concession from Defence safety policy or regulation is handled case by case, with MOD Legal Advisers, and only after other options have been considered and dismissed; the SRO/User sponsors the application. Routes differ by what is being exempted. For legislation, the application goes through JSP 815 Volume 2 Annex B, typically to the Secretary of State and sometimes to a DSA regulator or another appointed body, with guidance sought from DSA first. For Defence safety policy, the application goes to Dir DS; for JSP 376 exemptions, guidance comes from the ASC, and for other safety policies from DDS Policy Branch. For Defence safety regulations, the application goes to the appropriate DSA regulator. Where an exemption is granted, the original requirement stays recorded in the requirements document, with the reason it is not being met and the rationale for seeking the exemption.
