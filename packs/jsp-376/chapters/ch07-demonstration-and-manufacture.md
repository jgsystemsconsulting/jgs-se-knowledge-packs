# Chapter 7: Demonstration and Manufacture

## The Demonstration key policy statement

The source states the Demonstration policy in one sentence, quoted here from the key policy statement:

> During the Demonstration Phase, the SRO must make sure that evidence is obtained to demonstrate that the safety requirements will be met, and safety risks will be reduced to a level that is ALARP and Tolerable when the capability is due to enter service.

## Evidence and design change in Demonstration

Most of the detailed safety evidence is produced in this phase, and this is also where removing risk is cheapest: a design change that eliminates a hazard before manufacture costs less than the same change later. The SMP is implemented, and because trials are often complex and jointly staffed between MOD and contractor, a specific joint trials SMP sitting below the programme SMP, agreed by both parties, is worth considering. The SMP is extended to cover Manufacture, and where Demonstration and Manufacture run concurrently, the arrangements address the specific safety risks that concurrency creates. Where an OSA was required, its mitigations are implemented.

Evidence may show that a requirement will not be met, in part or in full. The safety impact is weighed beside all the other requirements, and where safety requirements are amended or removed the decision belongs to the individuals appointed for that purpose in the URD and SRD, recorded with a documented justification. The Legislative Compliance Assessment keeps running, and compliance evidence accumulates with it. Where legislation will not be met and an exemption is needed, an Exemption Case Submission goes to the Secretary of State, as early as possible once FBC approval is given, because assembling supplier information and obtaining a considered decision both take time.

## Test and evaluation in Demonstration

Enough evidence is gathered through the ITEAP to show that every safety requirement will be met. That evidence can come from calculation, simulation, test, inspection, production test, or operator trials, and the safety of the people conducting the T&E is itself a factor when the balance between routes is chosen. Activity at, or beyond, the edge of previous trials receives particular checking, so that design intention and material state are not compromised.

Responsibility splits into three cases. Where MOD controls the T&E, the T&E User owns a separate T&E safety case, demonstrates that the residual risks of all anticipated T&E are ALARP and tolerable, implements the supervision and control measures, and makes sure operators understand the safe-to-operate configuration, which can change as the design matures. Where a contractor controls the T&E but MOD personnel take part, the SRO keeps a duty of care, and the T&E User carries out a risk assessment covering the participating MOD staff before each activity, including assurance that the contractor made their own assessment. Where the T&E is contractor-only, MOD may carry little direct liability, but the legal and reputational risks remain real, and the SRO should still seek assurance that suitable and sufficient measures stand behind the activity. Certification evidence must remain valid for the production standard after later design changes.

## The safety case through Demonstration

The safety case is continually developed, and User involvement grows as T&E evidences the operating and maintenance procedures. A Demonstration safety case report should confirm that the required evidence has been gathered, that confidence is growing, and that the processes will support the ALARP and tolerable judgement; the SRO should sign it, and it is shared with the ASC if a formal submission is needed. There is no normal approving-authority submission at the end of Demonstration: the Annex C evidence is what tells the SRO the programme is ready to move, and where a formal decision is required the evidence is agreed with the ASC first.

## The Manufacture key policy statement

The source states the Manufacture policy in one sentence, quoted here from the key policy statement:

> During the Manufacture Phase, the SRO must make sure that the capability is delivered to the standards required, and that the User is satisfied on handover that the residual safety risks have been reduced to an ALARP and Tolerable level.

## Production, handover, and the In-Service Date

Neither the production process nor design changes may compromise safety. Much of the assessment to date rests on assumptions and pre-manufacture data, and once the complete system exists, trials verify those assumptions. The supporting arrangements, logistics and training among them, must be in place before service.

The In-Service Date is a two-way agreement. The SRO is satisfied that the in-service safety arrangements exist, including an in-service SMP construct, and that the User understands the residual risks. For its part, the User is satisfied that the solution is safe to operate, that they can operate it safely, and accepts the residual risk. All the information needed for safe operation is transferred in time for any further T&E before Initial Operating Capability, and where growth to Full Operating Capability is staged, the User understands the safety position at each stage.

Where the User is required to be a Duty Holder, formal appointment by the appropriate Senior Duty Holder must happen before responsibility is assumed, with DSA regulator endorsement where required. JSP 815 Element 5 covers Duty Holders; it is named here and not restated.

## Requirements, certification, and the final baseline

The final URD and SRD, safety requirements included, are handed to the User as the baseline for later development. The Legislative Compliance Assessment is completed before entry, with exemptions in place or an Exemption Case Submission already with the Secretary of State. Quality assurance runs against the production standard. Certification is complete before in-service use; where not every requirement can be met, a limited certificate with caveats is possible with the User's agreement, enduring caveats amend the URD, temporary ones need a funded programme to remove them, and a certifying body may impose a time limit beyond which certification lapses.

Design changes that touch safety are discussed with the User, go through risk assessment before agreement, and are reflected in the safety case. By entry to service the safety case covers all three aspects: the requirements, the design and manufacture, and the operating and maintenance arrangements. The SRO makes sure an In-Service Date safety case report is produced before the declaration; the User signs it to accept the safety case, and the SRO passes it to the ASC where a formal submission is called for. The SRO remains accountable until formal handover. The Safety Assurance Model continues. There is no normal submission at the end of Manufacture; the Annex C evidence is the readiness guide, and where a formal decision is required the evidence is agreed with the ASC first.
