# Human/AI Teams

## Core Idea

Defence expects humans and AI to work in teams, and the advantages of that teaming are central to applying AI across Defence: better overall effectiveness, optimal use of resources, and easier integration. Realising the benefit depends on understanding the relative strengths of humans and machines and how to combine them within a particular context of use. Teaming extends beyond the person at the interface: it covers the wider team of people supporting, training and maintaining the system, and as systems interconnect, individual teams grow into teams of human/AI teams across the Defence enterprise.

The human factors duty applies to every AI application, from combat systems to administrative and support systems. Delivering effective teams depends on a Human Centred Design (HCD) approach across the system lifecycle, which identifies user needs, involves users in design and testing, and applies human factors best practice; a well-managed HCD approach optimises performance, reduces risk, improves cost-effectiveness and supports user adoption.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Through-life HCD as a duty: a recognised HCD approach, appropriate to the system under development, must be applied across the lifecycle including introduction into service and in-service updates, resourced, managed and integrated within the wider software development process.
- The Human Factors Integration (HFI) process mandated by JSP 912 for military capability development and acquisition, with the caveat, named here, that JSP 912 and Def Stan 00-251 do not specifically address AI technologies.
- Functional analysis and allocation: a design step that decides which functions sit with the human and which with the machine, singled out as critical for human/AI teaming.
- Trust calibration through training: users must understand system behaviour, performance and limitations well enough to trust the system correctly under different use cases and conditions.

## Key Concepts

The through-life HCD approach must include identifying the users of the system and understanding their characteristics; identifying user needs and developing human-factors requirements with associated acceptance criteria for inclusion in project documentation; and involving users in developing and testing the system, including AI training and retraining. Human sciences insights should also be applied: established human factors principles and accepted best practice, suitable methods, tools, techniques and data, and principles of transparency, explainability and interpretability built into system and interface design so users can understand system behaviour.

The allocation analysis between human and AI agents, across all modes of function and levels of autonomy, is conducted to provide evidence of compliance with the ethical principles; to ensure there is no accountability gap, meaning humans remain accountable for, and in control of, the system's effects; to understand the human roles in system safety, performance, resilience and preventing AI bias; and to provide evidence supporting wider legal and regulatory arguments such as safety cases and legal reviews.

People implications run from the outset and keep being re-evaluated: AI technologies tend to augment rather than replace human tasks, but the workforce consequences, including personnel numbers and locations, positive or negative safety impacts, organisational structures, and changes in knowledge, skills and experience, must be identified and planned for.

Training implications follow the same through-life logic. Training must go beyond operating proficiency to build understanding of behaviour, performance and limitations, and must address a wide variety of scenarios including edge cases that stress the human-machine team and trigger reversionary ways of working. Training Needs Analysis should include the user's need to calibrate trust under different conditions. Where humans and AI both learn from each other's behaviours, collaborative training should be considered, and the overall behaviour of each team must be assured case by case.

## Mental Models

- Trust is an engineering output, not a feeling: it is produced by user involvement, explanation-capable interfaces, and training that deliberately exercises the system's limits.
- The accountability-gap test: if a proposed allocation cannot answer who remains accountable for an effect, the allocation is not done.
- Reversionary modes are part of the design, not an afterthought; training must rehearse them.

## Key Takeaways

- HCD, HFI and user involvement are lifecycle-long obligations, not concept-stage activities, and they continue across AI retraining.
- Allocation decisions are evidence generators: the same analysis that places functions also feeds ethical-principle compliance and legal arguments.
- Workforce and training implications are tracked from project outset, and competency needs are re-assessed when AI enters an existing role.
- JSP 912 carries the HFI mandate; its current text does not cover AI, which is why this directive restates the through-life duties in AI terms.
