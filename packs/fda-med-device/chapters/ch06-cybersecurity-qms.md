# Chapter 6: Cybersecurity and the QMS

Sources: S6 Cybersecurity in Medical Devices: Quality Management System Considerations and Content of Premarket Submissions (2026-02-03), §§ IV.A-IV.C (cybersecurity as part of safety and QMSR; SPDF as a path to satisfy QMSR; designing for security; transparency), V intro and V.A (using an SPDF; security risk management; third-party components and SBOM as configuration/QMS artifacts; unresolved anomalies; TPLC security risk management), VI.B (cybersecurity management plans), VII.A-VII.C.2 (cyber device duties under section 524B(b)(1)-(2) plans and reasonable-assurance processes). Premarket packing-list assembly is ch09; technical content depth for threat models, SBOM fields, controls, tests, and labeling substance is ch07.

## Core Idea

Cybersecurity is not a premarket appendix bolted onto an otherwise complete quality system. Under S6, cybersecurity is part of device safety and effectiveness and part of the QMSR: manufacturers must run development and lifecycle processes that identify security risks, set design requirements for controls, and produce evidence that controls work in the intended environment of use. A **Secure Product Development Framework (SPDF)** is FDA named way to structure those processes across design, development, release, support, and decommission so vulnerabilities are fewer and less severe. The SBOM is both a transparency artifact and a **QMS configuration-management artifact** that should be maintained and updated for marketed devices. Postmarket, the same system must monitor, assess, and mitigate new vulnerabilities, feed CAPA and complaint processes, and-for statutory **cyber devices**-carry section 524B plans and reasonable-assurance processes. This chapter is the process half of S6. What goes in the submission binder is ch09; what good threat models, SBOM fields, architecture, tests, and labels contain is ch07.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Cybersecurity inside QMSR (IV.A)**: QMS requirements in 21 CFR Part 820 incorporate ISO 13485. Premarket and postmarket stages can both pull on those requirements. S6 maps cybersecurity work onto design and development (Clause 7.3 family, including software validation under Subclause 7.3.7), risk management in product realization (Subclause 7.1), and postmarket QMS processes such as complaint handling (Subclause 8.2.2 with § 820.35(a)), quality audit (Subclause 8.2.4), analysis of data and improvement (Subclauses 8.4 and 8.5), and servicing (Subclause 7.5.4 with § 820.35(b)).
- **SPDF (IV.A.1, V)**: a set of processes that help identify and reduce the number and severity of vulnerabilities across the full product lifecycle. Using an SPDF is one approach to help ensure the QMSR is met; other frameworks can also satisfy QMSR if they align with FDA SPDF recommendations (examples S6 lists include medical-device Joint Security Plan material, IEC 81001-5-1, and ISA 62443-4-1-style product security development lifecycle requirements).
- **Security risk management as system-context work (V.A)**: assess safety and security risks of each device inside the larger system in which it operates; produce and maintain a security risk management report (threat model, cybersecurity risk assessment, SBOM and support information, vulnerability assessments, unresolved anomaly assessments, residual-risk conclusion, mitigations, traceability).
- **SBOM as QMS artifact (V.A.4)**: maintain an SBOM or equivalent capability as part of device configuration management; update it when marketed software changes; use it to support design-and-development files and medical device file documentation expectations under the incorporated QMS clauses S6 cites (Subclauses 7.3.10 and 4.2.3).
- **TPLC security risk management (V.A.6)**: keep identifying, assessing, and mitigating vulnerabilities throughout the supported lifecycle; refresh threat models and risk assessments as new threats appear; use that living documentation to drive timely CAPA under Subclause 8.5.
- **Cybersecurity management plan (VI.B; VII.C.1 for cyber devices)**: process machinery for vulnerability intake, coordinated disclosure, assessment, and update/patch response over time.
- **Section 524B process overlay for cyber devices (VII)**: statutory duties on submitters of 510(k), PMA, PDP, De Novo, or HDE for a cyber device to provide a postmarket vulnerability plan (b)(1), to design/develop/maintain processes providing reasonable assurance the device and related systems are cybersecure (b)(2), and to provide an SBOM (b)(3)-with (b)(3) content detailed in ch07 and packaging in ch09.

## Key Concepts

- **QMSR is the legal process home.** Device manufacturers must establish and follow a QMS under the QMSR. S6 states that cybersecurity analyses lean on software validation and risk management, and that development processes must account for software risks throughout design and development, which may include cybersecurity considerations. Example process content S6 expects: identify security risks; design requirements for how risks will be controlled; evidence that controls function as designed and are effective in the environment of use.
- **Premarket review is not a QMSR audit.** S6 is explicit that its recommendations are not a claim that FDA will evaluate QMSR compliance as part of a 510(k) substantial-equivalence decision. The guidance explains how cybersecurity **performance outputs** of QMSR-type activities are evaluated for safety and effectiveness, and how those outputs can be reused in the file.
- **Design-control hook for software devices.** S6 reminds readers that § 820.10(c) requires devices automated with computer software (among others) to comply with Design and Development Clause 7.3 and its subclauses, and that design and development validation includes validation of device software. Cybersecurity risk management and validation processes may be needed as part of Subclause 7.1 and Subclause 7.3.7 work where appropriate.
- **SPDF definition and scope.** An SPDF helps identify and reduce number and severity of vulnerabilities. It spans design, development, release, support, and decommission. Early SPDF use can avoid later re-engineering when connectivity is added or when vulnerabilities creating uncontrolled risk appear. Integrate SPDF with existing product, software, risk, and QMS processes rather than inventing a parallel shadow system. FDA encourages SPDF use; it does not claim SPDF is the only lawful path.
- **Primary SPDF goal.** Manufacture and maintain safe and effective devices that are, from a security standpoint, trustworthy and resilient, and that manufacturers and users can manage (install, configure, update, review logs) through design and labeling. Healthcare facilities may also manage devices inside frameworks such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework.
- **Designing for security (IV.B).** Security objectives and control thinking belong in design, not only in postmarket patches. S6 later architecture and control material (ch07) is the design content; the QMS obligation is to plan, input, output, verify, and validate security as design work under the Clause 7.3 process family.
- **Transparency (IV.C).** Users and FDA need enough security information to operate and review the device. Labeling substance is ch07; management plans and continuous SBOM availability are process commitments owned here.
- **Security risk management report as living QMS output (V.A).** Beyond storing artifacts, the report should summarize risk evaluation methods; state residual risk conclusions; detail mitigations; and provide traceability among threat model, cybersecurity risk assessment, SBOM, testing, and related documentation. That report is generated by process, then sliced for submissions (ch09).
- **Third-party software and custodial control (V.A.4).** Establish and maintain custodial control of device source code throughout the lifecycle as part of configuration management when possible (escrow, backups, or other methods). When licensing blocks source access, still plan how third-party components will be updated or replaced if support ends, and give users labeling information needed to manage component risk. Purchasing controls may need to secure future source access at supplier end-of-support.
- **SBOM as supply-chain and configuration tool.** A strong SBOM covers manufacturer-developed components, purchased/licensed software, open-source software, and upstream dependencies. It helps identify which devices and systems a newly disclosed vulnerability can touch, both while choosing components in development and after release. Because vulnerability management is critical, maintain the SBOM (or equivalent) in configuration management, update it when fielded software changes, and let it support QMS design/development and medical-device file documentation.
- **Postmarket / TPLC loop (V.A.6 and IV.A postmarket list).** Cybersecurity risks continue after release. Manufacturers need resources to identify, assess, and mitigate vulnerabilities across the supported lifecycle. Update security risk management documentation when new threats, vulnerabilities, assets, or impacts appear. Use that documentation to support timely corrective and preventive action. Complaint handling, quality audit, data analysis, improvement, software validation, risk management, and servicing are the QMSR process seats S6 names for complete postmarket cybersecurity programs (with a pointer to FDA separate Postmarket Cybersecurity guidance for general postmarket detail).
- **Cybersecurity management plans (VI.B).** Process descriptions for how the organisation monitors, receives, assesses, discloses, and responds to vulnerabilities and exploits, including update and patch mechanics. For cyber devices, section 524B(b)(1) makes a plan to monitor, identify, and address postmarket vulnerabilities and exploits-including coordinated vulnerability disclosure and related procedures-a submission requirement; S6 recommends that plan contain the management-plan substance in VI.B.
- **Cyber device process duties (VII.A-C).** A cyber device under section 524B(c) includes software validated, installed, or authorized as a device or in a device; has the ability to connect to the internet; and contains technological characteristics that could be vulnerable to cybersecurity threats. FDA reads software to include firmware and programmable logic, and ability to connect to the internet to include intentional or unintentional connectivity by any means on the threat surface. Submitters on the listed pathways must include information FDA requires under 524B(b). Process highlights: (b)(1) postmarket plan with CVD and related procedures; (b)(2) design, develop, and maintain processes and procedures providing reasonable assurance that the device and related systems are cybersecure, including regular-cycle updates/patches for known unacceptable vulnerabilities and out-of-cycle updates/patches as soon as possible for critical vulnerabilities that could cause uncontrolled risks (S6 ties those risk flavors to the Postmarket Cybersecurity guidance). SPDF-style processes in Section V are recommended helpers for (b)(2), not a complete SPDF cookbook.
- **Division of labor inside this pack.** ch06 = processes, SPDF, SBOM maintenance, postmarket/CAPA/monitoring, 524B plans and reasonable-assurance process duties. ch07 = threat modeling method, SBOM field content, security requirements/architecture/testing, labeling content. ch09 = how those outputs are assembled and scaled in a premarket file.

## Mental Models

- **Process generates evidence; submission samples it.** If the SPDF and QMS are weak, no amount of binder formatting in ch09 fixes the gap.
- **SPDF is a lifecycle operating system.** Design-time threat modeling without release, support, and decommission processes is not an SPDF.
- **SBOM is configuration management, not a PDF souvenir.** If the fielded software changes and the SBOM does not, vulnerability response will miss devices.
- **Postmarket is still design responsibility.** New vulnerability leads to update risk docs, CAPA/improvement, and patch/label/user communication as needed.
- **524B is a duty flag on cyber devices.** It adds statutory process and documentation floors; it does not replace QMSR or the rest of S6.

## Anti-patterns

- **Creating a cyber team outside design control, risk management, complaint handling, and CAPA.** S6 seats cybersecurity inside those QMS processes.
- **One-time premarket SBOM with no owner and no update path after clearance.**
- **Assuming ISO 13485 certification or an SPDF poster means 524B(b)(2) processes exist.** Show monitor/patch/disclose machinery that actually runs.
- **Waiting for a premarket deficiency letter to invent coordinated vulnerability disclosure.**
- **Treating hospital networks as trusted by process assumption.** Security risk management is system-context work; hostile-network assumptions belong in the threat model (ch07) and must be maintained over the TPLC.
- **Dumping entire QMS procedure manuals into every submission instead of maintaining controlled processes and submitting device-relevant outputs (ch09).**
- **Ignoring servicing and complaint record hooks when field cybersecurity incidents arrive as IT tickets.**

## Key Takeaways

1. Cybersecurity is part of safety/effectiveness and part of QMSR; software validation and risk-management processes must address security risks where appropriate.
2. An SPDF is a full-lifecycle set of processes to reduce vulnerability number and severity and is one way to help satisfy QMSR; integrate it with existing QMS processes.
3. Maintain security risk management documentation (threat model, assessments, SBOM, anomalies) as living TPLC artifacts that drive CAPA and improvement.
4. Treat the SBOM as a configuration-management and medical-device-file-supporting artifact, updated when marketed software changes.
5. Postmarket cybersecurity programs should use complaint handling, audit, data analysis, improvement, validation, risk management, and servicing seats in the QMS.
6. Cybersecurity management plans operationalize vulnerability intake, CVD, and update/patch response; they are mandatory plan content for cyber-device submissions under 524B(b)(1).
7. Cyber devices also need ongoing 524B(b)(2) processes that provide reasonable assurance of cybersecurity, including justified regular and emergency update cycles.
8. Leave submission assembly to ch09 and technical content depth to ch07; keep this chapter on process ownership.
