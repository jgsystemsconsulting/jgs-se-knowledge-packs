# Chapter 7: Cybersecurity Premarket Content

Sources: S6 Cybersecurity in Medical Devices: Quality Management System Considerations and Content of Premarket Submissions (2026-02-03), §§ IV.B-IV.D (designing for security; transparency; submission documentation scaling), V.A-V.C (threat modeling; cybersecurity risk assessment; interoperability; third-party components and SBOM content; unresolved anomalies; security architecture and controls; cybersecurity testing), VI.A (labeling recommendations for devices with cybersecurity risks), VII.B-VII.C.3 and VII.E (cyber device definition touchpoints; 524B documentation content for plans, reasonable assurance, and SBOM; reasonable assurance framing). QMS/SPDF process ownership is ch06; submission packing order and Appendix 4 assembly are ch09.

## Core Idea

When FDA reviews a device with cybersecurity considerations, it is reading a **content story**: what could go wrong in the system, what you built to stop or contain it, how you know those controls work, what users must do, and-especially for cyber devices-whether statutory SBOM and process evidence is present. This chapter is S6 premarket **content** half: threat modeling expectations, cybersecurity risk assessment logic, SBOM field and supportability content, security architecture and control requirements, security testing types, and labeling substance. It is not the QMS playbook (ch06) and not the binder packing list (ch09). Build the substance here so ch06 processes have something real to maintain and ch09 has something coherent to ship.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Threat modeling (V.A.1)**: process to identify security objectives, risks, and vulnerabilities across the medical device system and define countermeasures across the lifecycle; foundational input to cybersecurity risk assessment and control selection.
- **Cybersecurity risk assessment (V.A.2)**: links threat scenarios to safety and effectiveness impact, risk acceptance, and controls; coordinated with, but not identical to, general medical device risk management; includes risk-transfer thinking when residual risk is shifted to users or environments.
- **Third-party / SBOM content model (V.A.4; VII.C.3)**: machine-readable SBOM with NTIA minimum elements plus support-level and end-of-support data per component; known-vulnerability assessments and controls; OTS guidance coordination (ch08).
- **Security architecture and controls (V.B, Appendix 1 categories)**: end-to-end definition of system connections and how they are secured; control categories spanning authentication, authorization, cryptography, integrity, confidentiality, logging, resiliency/recovery, and update mechanisms.
- **Cybersecurity testing (V.C)**: testing beyond ordinary software V&V to show controls work in a security context (requirements tests, threat-mitigation tests, vulnerability testing including abuse cases, fuzzing, attack-surface analysis, composition analysis, and related methods).
- **Labeling for cybersecurity risks (VI.A)**: user-facing security information as a risk-management and misbranding-prevention tool under sections 502(f) and 502(a).
- **524B content floor for cyber devices (VII.C)**: plan content (b)(1), reasonable-assurance process evidence (b)(2), SBOM (b)(3) with V.A.4.b substance.

## Key Concepts

- **Threat modeling is system-wide and continuous in design.** Perform threat modeling to inform risk analysis; do it throughout design; include all medical device system elements. The threat model should identify system risks and mitigations and inform pre- and post-mitigation risks in the cybersecurity risk assessment; state assumptions about the system or environment of use (S6 example posture: treat hospital networks as inherently hostile and assume an adversary can alter, drop, and replay packets); and capture cybersecurity risks introduced through supply chain, manufacturing, deployment, interoperation with other devices, maintenance/update activities, and decommissioning pathways called out in S6.
- **Security risk management report content spine (V.A).** For demonstrating reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness, S6 expects documentation elements covering system threat modeling, cybersecurity risk assessment, SBOM, component support information, vulnerability assessments, and unresolved anomaly assessment(s). The report should also summarize methods, residual-risk conclusions, mitigations, and traceability to testing and related artifacts. Architecture may be part of the same process story but is discussed separately in S6 because of the review detail FDA wants (ch09 handles view packaging; this chapter handles what those views must communicate).
- **Cybersecurity risk assessment (V.A.2).** Assess risks in system context. Acceptance criteria should consider the total product life cycle, not only day-zero. Distinguish safety risk management from security risk management while keeping them connected: a security exploit can create or magnify safety harm. Document risk transfers to users or environments and ensure transferred tasks are realistic (human factors link under labeling).
- **Interoperability considerations (V.A.3).** Interfaces and data exchanges expand the threat surface. Threat models and architecture must include expected interoperability relationships and the security properties of those exchanges, not only the standalone device box.
- **Third-party software content (V.A.4).** Provide plans for update or replacement when third-party support ends; give users labeling information on known vulnerabilities, configuration, and related risk management. Pair SBOM work with OTS/SOUP expectations (ch08) when the manufacturer lacks full lifecycle control of a component.
- **SBOM content requirements (V.A.4(a)-(b)).** Include manufacturer-developed components, third-party purchased/licensed software, open-source software, and upstream dependencies. For premarket review, provide **machine-readable** SBOMs consistent with the NTIA minimum elements / baseline attributes in Framing Software Component Transparency: Establishing a Common Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) (October 2021). **Additionally, for each component**, include software level of support (e.g., actively maintained, no longer maintained, abandoned) and end-of-support date-either inside the SBOM or in a separate addendum. Industry-accepted SBOM formats are encouraged. If SBOM information cannot be provided, justify why. Also identify known vulnerabilities associated with the device and its software components, including those in CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog; describe how vulnerabilities were discovered (method robustness); and for each known vulnerability provide a safety and security risk assessment (device and system impacts) plus details of applicable controls, including compensating controls.
- **Cyber devices and SBOM statute (VII.C.3).** Section 524B(b)(3) requires cyber-device manufacturers to provide an SBOM in the covered premarket submissions. S6 states that SBOM documentation should contain the information recommended in Section V.A.4.b. For non-cyber devices, SBOM documentation remains part of the recommended third-party software story even without that statutory floor.
- **Unresolved anomalies with security impact (V.A.5).** Start from the S1 anomaly list habit (ch02). Evaluate anomalies for security implications and possible vulnerability status as part of a complete security risk assessment under Subclause 7.1. Consider CWE categories. An anomaly that looks sporadic in normal use may be continuously exploitable under adversarial use; security-minded assessment can change acceptability. Provide criteria and rationales for addressing anomalies with security impacts in the premarket documentation.
- **Security architecture (V.B).** Manufacturers must identify cybersecurity risks in devices and expected operating systems (hospital networks, cloud, other functions per multiple-function policy, and similar) and implement appropriate controls. A security architecture defines the system and end-to-end connections into and out of it: high-level interacting devices/systems plus detailed implementation of how interactions occur and are secured. It should demonstrate that risks from risk management are adequately controlled.
- **Design-control hooks for architecture (V.B).** S6 ties architecture work to design and development planning, inputs, and outputs under Subclauses 7.3.1-7.3.4: procedures, plans with responsibilities, requirements inputs for intended use, and outputs suitable for verification with acceptance criteria and essential safe-use outputs identified. Premarket submissions should include security architecture documentation. Implementations may vary; security objectives from Section IV still apply.
- **Security control categories (V.B.1, Appendix 1).** Controls commonly cluster into authentication; authorization; cryptography; code, data, and execution integrity; confidentiality; event detection and logging; resiliency and recovery; firmware and software updates. Select and implement controls from the risk and architecture story, not from a decorative checklist. Evidence must show controls enforce the intended security policy and perform under relevant conditions.
- **Architecture views substance (V.B.2, Appendix 2).** Global system view; multi-patient harm view; updateability/patchability view; security use case views for functionality with safety risks. Views answer different questions (trust boundaries and data flows; blast radius across patients; how the device is patched in the field; how a safety-relevant function behaves under attack or misuse). Diagram and information-detail expectations are expanded in Appendix 2; ch09 covers how many views to ship at IDE vs marketing depth.
- **Cybersecurity testing content (V.C).** Ordinary software V&V is not enough. Under design verification and validation expectations (Subclauses 7.3.6 and 7.3.7), include security testing of the medical device system. S6 recommends considering, among other types: **security requirements testing** (evidence each design input was implemented; boundary analysis and rationale for boundary assumptions); **threat mitigation testing** (evidence risk controls work per threat models for global, multi-patient harm, updatability/patchability, and security use case views; adequacy of each control for policy enforcement, traffic, stability, reliability as appropriate); **vulnerability testing** (abuse/misuse cases and malformed inputs; robustness; fuzz testing; attack surface analysis; vulnerability chaining; closed-box known-vulnerability scanning; software composition analysis of binaries; and related analyses referenced to practices such as those in ANSI/ISA 62443-4-1). Submit security testing documentation and associated reports/assessments.
- **Labeling content for cybersecurity risks (VI.A).** Labeling must support adequate directions for use (section 502(f)) and must not be false or misleading (section 502(a)). For devices with cybersecurity risks, informing users of relevant security information can be both a compliance path and a design risk-control. Depth, location (operator manual vs security implementation guide), and delivery method should match the intended audience, including patients or caregivers with limited technical knowledge. If using online portals, keep links current and accurate. Risks transferred to users should be detailed and considered as usability/human-factors tasks.
- **Illustrative labeling elements (VI.A examples).** Device instructions and product specifications for recommended cybersecurity controls in the intended environment (e.g., anti-malware, firewall, password requirements); sufficiently detailed diagrams so users can implement those controls; network ports and other interfaces expected to send/receive data, with port functionality, direction, and approved destination end-points; supporting infrastructure requirements (minimum networking, supported encryption interfaces) and technical instructions for secure deployment/servicing and user response to vulnerabilities or incidents; SBOM availability to users on a continuous basis in a machine-readable format; systematic procedures for users to download version-identifiable manufacturer-authorized software/firmware and how users learn updates exist; how design responds to anomalous conditions (user notification and logging of configuration changes, network anomalies, login attempts, anomalous traffic); high-level description of features that protect critical functionality (backup modes, disabling interfaces, and similar). Full enumeration and placement strategy still scale with device and user type; ch09 covers file placement.
- **Cyber device identification (VII.B) as a content trigger.** Software (including firmware/programmable logic) + ability to connect to the internet (intentional or not, any means on the threat surface) + technological characteristics that could be vulnerable to cyber threats. Brief connections (e.g., service USB) can still count as ability to connect. When the flag is on, 524B content floors apply on top of general S6 recommendations.
- **524B(b)(1) plan content.** Plan to monitor, identify, and address postmarket vulnerabilities and exploits in a reasonable time, including CVD and related procedures. S6 recommends the plan address coordinated disclosure of externally identified and manufacturer-identified vulnerabilities/exploits and procedures to carry disclosures out (informing users, customers, patients, and other relevant parties as appropriate). The plan should also describe timelines and justifications for developing and releasing updates/patches, aligned with 524B(b)(2)(A) regular-cycle updates for known unacceptable vulnerabilities and 524B(b)(2)(B) as-soon-as-possible out-of-cycle updates for critical vulnerabilities that could cause uncontrolled risks.
- **524B(b)(2) content.** Show processes and procedures that provide reasonable assurance the device and related systems are cybersecure. Section V SPDF recommendations are one content library for that showing; ch06 owns keeping those processes alive.
- **Reasonable assurance framing (VII.E and IV).** Cybersecurity documentation is part of demonstrating reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness for devices with cybersecurity risks. Inadequate cybersecurity labeling can support misbranding concerns. Alternative approaches are possible if they still meet statutory and regulatory requirements; S6 outlines are the Agency detailed recommendations from review experience.
- **What not to duplicate here.** ch09 owns Appendix 4/Table 1 packing order, IDE vs marketing scaling of **which** artifacts are recommended, and eSTAR placement pointers. ch06 owns SPDF operation, SBOM maintenance, and postmarket CAPA machinery. This chapter owns whether the threat model, SBOM fields, controls, tests, and labels are substantively adequate.

## Mental Models

- **Assets, adversaries, interfaces, abuse paths.** If the threat model cannot name those, controls are decoration.
- **Hostile network is a default design assumption unless you can justify a stronger boundary.** S6 example assumption is intentionally harsh.
- **SBOM without support metadata is half a bill of materials.** Reviewers need support level and end-of-support, not only package names.
- **Security testing is not software V&V renamed.** Adversarial and abuse-case methods are in scope.
- **Labeling is a control and a legal face.** Transferred risk that users cannot perform is not real transfer.

## Anti-patterns

- **Threat model that stops at the device Ethernet port and ignores cloud, update servers, manufacturer tools, and supply chain.**
- **SBOM as a static spreadsheet of package names without machine-readable format, support data, or known-exploited vulnerability analysis.**
- **Control catalog copied from Appendix 1 with no mapping to threat scenarios or architecture views.**
- **Only positive-path software tests offered as cybersecurity testing.**
- **Security use case views missing for safety-critical functions while global network diagrams look polished.**
- **Labeling that says secure network required without ports, infrastructure specs, update procedures, or incident response instructions.**
- **For cyber devices, omitting 524B SBOM or management-plan substance while shipping a narrative essay.**
- **Contradicting ch02 anomaly or architecture statements in the cyber section.**

## Key Takeaways

1. Build a system-scoped threat model with explicit environment assumptions, lifecycle risk sources, and linkage to pre/post-mitigation cybersecurity risk assessment.
2. Produce a security risk management content set: threat model, risk assessment, SBOM plus support metadata, vulnerability assessments, unresolved security anomalies, residual risk, mitigations, and traceability to tests.
3. Ship machine-readable SBOMs meeting NTIA minimum elements, plus per-component support level and end-of-support; assess known vulnerabilities (including KEV catalog entries) with safety/security impact and controls.
4. Define security architecture end-to-end and implement controls across the Appendix 1 categories as driven by risk; support the story with the architecture views S6 describes.
5. Perform and document cybersecurity testing beyond standard software V&V: requirements, threat mitigation, and vulnerability testing families.
6. Write labeling that gives the intended user actionable security information (controls, ports/interfaces, infrastructure, SBOM access, update procedures, anomaly response, critical-function protections) and treat transferred risk as a human-factors problem.
7. For cyber devices, ensure 524B plan, reasonable-assurance process, and SBOM content floors are met with V.A.4.b SBOM substance.
8. Hand process ownership to ch06 and file assembly/scaling to ch09; keep this chapter on technical content adequacy.
