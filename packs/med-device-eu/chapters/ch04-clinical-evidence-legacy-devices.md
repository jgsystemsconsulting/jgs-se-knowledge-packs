# What MDCG 2020-6 counts as sufficient clinical evidence for legacy devices

S2 answers a migration question: a device CE marked under the old directives (MDD or AIMDD) now faces conformity assessment under the MDR, and the notified body will ask what clinical evidence supports it.
The guidance calls these products legacy devices and sets the standard for the clinical evaluation that must accompany them.

## The core answer

Sufficient clinical evidence is not a fixed dataset.
S2 defines it as the present result of a qualified assessment that has concluded the device is safe and achieves its intended clinical benefits, where the underlying data and the evaluation of that data allow a qualified reviewer to reach that conclusion.
The assessment is continuous, and the manufacturer must specify and justify the level of clinical evidence the device needs, judged against the device's characteristics and its intended purpose.

For a legacy device the evidence base is the union of two streams: the pre-market and post-market clinical data generated under the old directives, and post-market data gathered since.
Data from the directive era does not carry over at full strength.
The MDR defines clinical data more narrowly than the directives did, its equivalence rules are stricter, and the state of the art has moved, so S2 expects a gap analysis against the MDR's general safety and performance requirements rather than a repackaging of the old evaluation.

## What the evaluation must do

S2 walks the five stages the MDR sets for clinical evaluation, adapted to legacy devices:

- Plan: identify the GSPRs needing clinical data, fix the intended purpose, target groups, indications, and contraindications, describe intended benefits with measurable outcome parameters, and set the benefit-risk parameters by reference to the state of the art in medicine.
- Identify data: pre-market sources (investigations of the device, studies of equivalent devices, peer-reviewed literature, case reports with a bias warning) and post-market sources (PMS and complaint data, PMCF studies, independent studies, registries, literature).
- Appraise: grade each dataset for methodological quality and bias with validated tools (Cochrane's risk-of-bias tool, MINORS, the Reisch tool, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale).
- Generate new data only where gaps cannot be bridged otherwise, and not as a routine: S2 states the MDR demonstration should not rely on new PMCF studies started under the MDR to bridge gaps such as unsupported indications.
- Analyse: benefits against risks against alternative treatments and the current state of the art, ending in a conformity conclusion, an explicit list of gaps, and a determination of PMCF needs.

Two rules in the appraisal stage carry most of the weight.
Complaint and incident rates cannot carry the safety argument on their own; S2 says the use of estimates such as "[number of incidents or complaints] / [number of device sales] cannot generally be considered sufficient to provide proof of safety".
And where the evidence will not support the claimed intended purpose, the fix is to narrow the intended purpose until the available evidence does support it.

S2 also gives the minimum content of the clinical evaluation plan for a legacy device:

- The GSPRs requiring clinical data, the intended purpose, and the target groups with indications and contraindications.
- Intended benefits with specified clinical outcome parameters, and a strategy to identify and assess alternative treatments.
- Methods for examining clinical safety, with reference to residual risks and side-effects.
- Benefit-risk acceptability parameters anchored in the state of the art in medicine.
- A strategy to identify, appraise, and analyse all available clinical data under the MDR's narrower clinical data definition, plus equivalence evidence where equivalence is claimed.
- The defined level of clinical evidence, and the strategy for collecting and assessing post-market surveillance data on the legacy device.

## Relief valves and the evidence hierarchy

For well-established technologies (simple, stable designs; a generic device group with known safety and standard-of-care status; a long market history), S2 allows a lower level of clinical evidence, potentially resting on post-market surveillance data gathered under a quality system.
It sets boundaries: reliance solely on complaints and vigilance is not sufficient, and equivalence must meet the MDR's criteria.
The non-clinical route (conformity shown without clinical data) exists but is closed to class III and implantable devices and is exceptional for everything else.

The guidance's appendix ranks evidence roughly from strongest to weakest: high-quality investigations covering the device's variants and indications; investigations with justified gaps; registry outcomes; studies with methodological flaws but quantifiable results; equivalence data; state-of-the-art and similar-device evaluation; complaints and vigilance data; proactive PMS; individual case reports; and finally non-clinical evidence such as bench testing.
Class III and implantable legacy devices that are not well-established technologies should have clinical data at least as strong as the fourth rank.
Low-risk standard-of-care devices with little evolution in the state of the art sit at the other end and may justify a more limited dataset.
