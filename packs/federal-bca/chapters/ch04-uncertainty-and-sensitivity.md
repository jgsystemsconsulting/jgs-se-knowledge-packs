# Chapter 4: Treatment of Uncertainty

## Core Idea
A-94 requires analysts to characterize uncertainty, use expected values where appropriate, conduct sensitivity analysis, and avoid opaque 'uncertainty adjustments' that hide assumptions. Uncertainty treatment is part of decision quality, not an appendix afterthought.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Characterize uncertainty**: State key uncertain parameters, relationships, and scenarios qualitatively and quantitatively.
- **Expected values**: When probabilities are supportable, summarize uncertain outcomes with expected values rather than only best-case points.
- **Sensitivity analysis**: Show how results change when important assumptions vary; identify switching values that flip rankings.
- **Adjustments for uncertainty**: Any risk adjustments must be explicit and justified—not buried inside inflated discount rates without discussion.

## Key Concepts
- **Parameter vs model uncertainty**: Distinguish uncertain inputs from uncertain structural form of the analysis.
- **Correlated risks**: Treat joint variation when benefits and costs move together.
- **Irreversibility and option value**: When decisions close future options, note implications for timing and information value.
- **Monte Carlo / scenarios**: Use quantitative tools when they clarify decisions; keep outputs decision-relevant, not decorative.
- **Transparency**: Document data sources, distributions, and omitted uncertainties that could bias results.

## Mental Models
- **A single-point NPV without sensitivity is a false precision claim.**
- **Hiding risk inside a higher discount rate often confounds time preference with risk.**

## Anti-patterns
- Reporting only the preferred scenario's NPV.
- Using 'conservative' stacked assumptions that bias against (or for) a program without disclosure.
- Skipping uncertainty because 'the circular is about discount rates only'—it is not.

## Key Takeaways
1. Characterize and, where possible, quantify uncertainty.
2. Prefer expected values plus sensitivity over opaque point estimates.
3. Make risk adjustments explicit.
4. Show which uncertainties could change the decision.
