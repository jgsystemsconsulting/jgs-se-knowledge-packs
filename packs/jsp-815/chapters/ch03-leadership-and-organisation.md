## Core Idea

Element 1 makes leadership, governance, and culture the front of the Defence SMS: tone from the top, clear accountabilities, visible leaders, safety held equal with other strategic objectives, and a culture that supports speaking up and resilient safety management. Element 2 turns that intent into organisation: Defence Area safety management arrangements, roles and resources, contractor standards, workforce consultation, change control, and dependency management. Annex A settles which Area's arrangements apply when people sit outside their parent Full Command structure.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Tone from the top under the SofS Policy Statement (E1 paragraphs 3 to 6).** The Policy Statement is personal SofS leadership and relies on every leadership role to apply it. Senior leaders of the four Defence Areas lead on safety through letters of delegation and/or terms of reference. The most senior leader of each Defence Area must set out health and safety responsibilities and arrangements; Area arrangements must reflect the Defence SMS and must not contradict Defence-level safety policy.
- **Defence Safety Committee and Area governance (E1 paragraphs 7 to 9).** The Defence Safety Committee is the principal Defence forum for safety governance. Each Defence Area must establish its own governance arrangements and framework so leadership can oversee safety performance, resources, and risk.
- **PDCA inside leadership practice (E1 paragraphs 10 to 11).** HSE's Plan, Do, Check, Act pattern is the basic shape for consistent safety management activity; Element 1 gives examples of leadership activities on that cycle (Figure described in source; not reproduced here).
- **Safety culture as lived practice (E1 paragraphs 12 to 22).** Culture is "the way we do things around here." Element 1 points at the HSE description of safety culture (third-party text; not reproduced in this pack) and requires arrangements that promote openness, reporting, learning, and engagement. The Parker-Hudson Ladder is named as a framework for assessing culture maturity; level definitions stay in the source.
- **Safety management arrangements (E2 paragraphs 3 to 5).** JSP 815 is the overarching SMS for Defence. Under the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, employers need arrangements for planning, organisation, control, monitoring, and review. Defence Areas must develop and maintain arrangements specific to their activities. Those arrangements may be a subordinate SMS aligned to JSP 815 or a simpler statement of organisation and arrangements; JSP 815 calls both "safety management arrangements."
- **Annex A applicability principles.** When units or people sit outside parent Full Command arrangements, Annex A sets which rulebook and whose assurance apply (FULLCOM parent vs host OPCOM vs lodge vs CSOC), with MOUs for residual cases.

## Key Concepts

### Element 1 Expectations (1.1 to 1.6)

| Exp | Theme (paraphrase) | Performance Statement themes (summary) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Leadership sets tone from the top and actively shows commitment to safety | Evidence that leaders own injury and ill-health prevention, prioritise safety in decisions, and make commitment visible in forums and communications |
| 1.2 | Leadership promotes continual improvement, speaking up, and open transparent reporting | Evidence of reporting channels used without blame for good-faith reports, improvement actions tracked, and learning fed back |
| 1.3 | Leadership sets clear safety responsibilities against which the Defence Area is measured and held to account | Documented roles, measures, and accountability routes that leadership actually uses |
| 1.4 | Leadership is visible at all levels, including direct interaction with the workforce and stakeholders on safety | Site visits, forums, and two-way engagement on safety topics, not only written policy |
| 1.5 | Corporate governance holds safety equal to other strategic objectives (capability, cost, schedule) | Governance agendas, resource bids, and decisions that treat safety as a peer objective |
| 1.6 | Culture fosters resilient safety management, engages people, and promotes effective safety behaviours | Arrangements for engagement, behaviours, and culture measurement or review |

Leadership commitment examples in Element 1 include taking overall responsibility for preventing work-related injury and ill health and providing safe workplaces; being visible and prioritising safety beside other strategic objectives; communicating the importance of conforming to the Defence SMS and Area arrangements; reviewing safety performance and bidding for resources; leading culture that supports SMS outcomes; consulting and training across the diversity of the workforce; encouraging improvement ideas; and promoting open reporting of occurrences, hazards, and risks (paragraph 6 items a to h, summarised).

### Element 2 Expectations (2.1 to 2.8)

| Exp | Theme (paraphrase) | Performance Statement themes (summary) |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Develop and maintain Area-specific safety management arrangements that show how the Defence SMS, policy, and regulations are delivered locally | Current, owned arrangements document (or subordinate SMS) matched to activities |
| 2.2 | Define safety roles, responsibilities, and accountabilities in those arrangements | Role definitions people can find and that match practice |
| 2.3 | System to allocate appropriate resources (budget and people) | Resource decisions traceable to safety needs and plans |
| 2.4 | Arrangements to share information about safety risks for effective risk management and continual improvement | Information flows across teams and interfaces |
| 2.5 | Check that contractor and supplier safety management standards meet or exceed Defence standards | Due diligence and ongoing checks on supply chain safety standards |
| 2.6 | Joint consultation mechanisms with workforce, contractors, and supply chain | Working consultation forums and evidence they influence decisions |
| 2.7 | Organisational structure or specialist-personnel changes evaluated, risk assessed, approved, and documented | Change records, risk assessment, and approval before/as changes land |
| 2.8 | Mechanisms to identify functional and organisational dependencies and interfaces and how safety risks are managed across them | Dependency maps, interface agreements, shared risk controls |

Minimum content Element 2 expects in Area arrangements (paragraph 5, summarised): continuous improvement and performance monitoring; safe systems of work so stakeholders know their roles; training, information, instruction, and supervision; extent of delegated authority and competence assessment; risk identification and management (detail in Element 4); consultation including trade unions and staff associations; safety governance structure, membership, and assurance; access to independent specialist advice; event reporting and management (detail in Element 10); and, where appropriate, detailed arrangements for further specialist needs.

### Annex A: whose SEMS applies

Purpose: Defence often embeds or assigns units and individuals across Areas with different arrangements; Annex A reduces confusion about applicability (paragraphs 1 to 3).

Direction principles (paragraph 5, paraphrase):

- **Assigned / visiting:** keep parent Defence Area arrangements; comply with host Standing Orders; host supports.
- **Embedded under OPCOM (formed, mixed, or singleton):** follow host Defence Area arrangements unless a formal inter-Area agreement says otherwise.
- **Tri-service or single-Service units embedded in a Defence Area structure:** maintain that host Defence Area's arrangements unless a formal agreement says otherwise.
- **Lodging units** (share a site under ADCON/LOGCON without HoE rights as defined in the annex): keep FULLCOM parent arrangements; still comply with host Standing Orders.
- **CSOC (including joint ops) OPCOM or postings:** adhere to CSOC policy and arrangements.
- **Lead for arrangements also leads assurance**, directly or by formal agreement where specialist competence sits elsewhere (examples named: Aviation, OME, Port Ops).

Cases outside these principles need a bilateral MOU covering audit and inspection. Duty Holding and assurance follow the same principles (paragraphs 6 to 7).

## Mental Models

- **Culture is downstream of leadership behaviour.** Policies without visible leaders, equal billing in governance, and safe reporting channels do not create Expectation 1.6 outcomes.
- **Arrangements are the local operating system.** JSP 815 is the Department SMS; Element 2 arrangements are how a Defence Area boots that SMS on its own hardware.
- **Host vs parent is a command question, not a courtesy.** Annex A keys off FULLCOM, OPCOM, lodge, and CSOC, then assurance follows the lead arrangements.
- **Contractors are inside the organisation problem.** Expectation 2.5 and 2.6 put supply-chain standards and consultation in the same Element as internal roles and resources.

## Anti-patterns

- Publishing a safety policy statement with no governance forum, no resource trail, and no leader time on the ground (fails 1.1, 1.4, 1.5 together).
- Building Area arrangements that contradict Defence-level policy or omit the minimum content list in Element 2 paragraph 5.
- Letting embedded OPCOM units keep parent rules by habit when Annex A says host rules apply (or the reverse for lodgers).
- Treating contractor safety as "their SMS, not ours" without standards checks or consultation routes.
- Moving structure or specialist people without evaluating safety impact (2.7) or ignoring cross-Area interfaces (2.8).
- Quoting HSE culture definitions from Element 1 as if this pack reproduced them; use the HSE source if the definition text is required.

## Key Takeaways

1. Element 1 Expectations 1.1 to 1.6: tone, speaking up, clear accountabilities, visibility, safety as equal strategic partner, resilient culture.
2. Element 2 Expectations 2.1 to 2.8: local arrangements, roles, resources, risk information sharing, contractor standards, consultation, change control, dependencies.
3. Annex A decides which Area's safety management arrangements and assurance apply across FULLCOM, OPCOM, lodge, and CSOC patterns; MOUs cover gaps.
4. Leadership owns both the culture story and the resource story; organisation without leadership visibility is incomplete, and tone without arrangements is empty.
5. Performance Statements for these Expectations describe evidence quality across assurance levels; cite Expectation numbers, not PS cells.
