## Core Idea

Element 3 requires every Defence Area to know which safety law, Defence policy, and Defence regulation applies, to keep Area policy consistent with Defence policy, to communicate requirements, to review guidance when things change, and to run a controlled process for exemptions, waivers, and concessions. Annex B is the Secretary of State exemption certificate route: last resort, evidence-heavy, time-limited, and conditioned. Statutory regulators and the Defence Safety Authority (DSA) regulators sit beside each other; Crown bodies face a different enforcement path from private employers, but duty of care does not vanish.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Compliance unless DED (E3 paragraph 3).** In the UK, Defence must comply with applicable safety legislation unless a disapplication, exemption, or derogation (DED) covers the case. That includes legislation giving effect to international obligations. Defence Areas must monitor new and changing statutory and regulatory requirements and manage compliance risk.
- **HSWA general duties and ALARP / SFAIRP language (E3 paragraphs 4 to 7).** HSWA Part 1 sections 2 and 3 set employer duties for employees and for others affected. The Act uses "so far as is reasonably practicable" (SFAIRP). Defence more often says ALARP for the process of lowering risk; both rest on "reasonably practicable." SofS is treated as the employer; the Policy Statement commits to UK compliance at home and, overseas, host-state law where it applies, with UK standards so far as reasonably practicable where host requirements fall short.
- **JSP 375 as minimum workplace standard (E3 paragraph 5).** JSP 375 sets how Defence will comply with UK health and safety legislation and is the minimum standard across Defence. Area organisational arrangements must at least match it and may improve on it. This pack names JSP 375; it does not carry JSP 375 content.
- **Crown enforcement path (E3 paragraphs 8 to 10).** MOD is subject to HSWA, but prosecution of the Department differs from private parties (HSWA section 48). Statutory regulators may use Crown Improvement Notices, Crown Prohibition Notices, or Crown Censure. Sections 33 to 42 do not bind the Crown as such but apply to persons in the public service of the Crown as they apply to others, so individuals may still face prosecution.
- **Defence arrangements where law does not reach (E3 paragraphs 11 to 12).** Where legislation does not apply, Defence keeps Departmental arrangements intended to be at least as good as UK legislation so far as practicable. DDSS policy plus DSA regulation under a DED are the Defence arrangements for those gaps; overarching duty of care continues worldwide.
- **Dir-DSS policy ownership and Area policy (E3 paragraphs 25 to 29).** Dir-DSS develops and maintains Defence safety policy. Areas build local arrangements that reflect their activities and must not conflict with Defence policy. Policy simplification is the default: avoid proliferating conflicting Area rules.
- **Statutory regulators and DSA regulators (E3 paragraphs 32 to 45).** Statutory regulators hold enforcement powers from legislation. DG DSA owns, develops, and maintains Defence safety regulations on behalf of SofS and, under the DSA Charter, empowers suitably qualified Crown servants to regulate Defence safety activity. Defence regulations exist for DEDs, for activity outside normal statutory reach, and for related Defence-specific needs listed in Element 3.
- **Annex B SofS exemption certificate process.** Used only when SofS has power to exempt and the case is justified by national security or direct compromise of operational capability after other avenues are exhausted.

### Eight Defence regulators (E3 paragraph 45, titles and remits as Element 3 lists them)

1. **Defence Environmental Protection Regulator (DEPR):** assurance, regulation, enforcement, and coordinated EP compliance view.
2. **Defence Fire Safety Regulator (DFSR):** fire safety and Fire and Rescue Services.
3. **Defence Land Safety Regulator (DLSR):** land systems, fuels, gases, movements and transport, adventurous training.
4. **Defence Maritime Regulator (DMR):** MOD shipping, ports and harbours, Defence diving.
5. **Defence Medical Services Regulator (DMSR):** patient and staff safety in Defence-delivered healthcare.
6. **Defence Nuclear Safety Regulator (DNSR):** nuclear activities and radiological safety of the Defence Nuclear Enterprise.
7. **Defence Ordnance, Munitions and Explosives Safety Regulator (DOSR):** OME, land ranges safety policy, major accident control regulations (MACR).
8. **Military Aviation Authority (MAA):** air safety across Defence.

Responsibilities are set out in their respective Defence regulations (not in this pack).

### Annex B: exemption certificate process (Expectations 3.5 in action)

**When:** SofS (or other named approving authorities) may grant an exemption from specified health and safety legislation provisions for a person or class of persons. Other approving authorities exist (HSE, Defra, DfT, MCA, local authorities, and separate routes such as the Land Exemption Committee for certain operational military vehicle construction-and-use issues). Mixed health-and-safety and environmental cases need early DDSS and specialist advice (DEPR, QSEE, or other competent authorities). Sponsors engage DDSS in all cases (Annex B paragraphs 1 to 2).

**Last resort tests (paragraph 3):** SofS must have legal power to exempt; the Exemption Case Submission (ECS) must show the activity, equipment, vehicles, or materials are required in the interests of national security or that compliance would directly compromise operational capability; all other avenues to fix non-compliance are exhausted. Certificates are normally time-limited (maximum five years unless a longer period is justified) and conditioned.

**Residual risk (paragraphs 4 to 5):** control measures must keep risk ALARP and tolerable; the exemption is granted only when stipulated conditions are satisfied and outcomes are shown to be safe.

**ECS content (paragraph 5, summarised):** full legislation title and specific sections or regulations; national-security or operational-capability evidence tied to the exemption power; persons affected; data-protection-compliant personal data handling; groups at different risk and extra controls considered; non-compliant equipment/material/activity and intended use; period and rationale; reasons and magnitude of non-compliance; actions already tried; cost data and CBA if cost is the barrier; evidence-based action plan with mitigations, costs, and timescales; health monitoring plan where applicable; links to supporting evidence; for renewals, results of the previous plan and monitoring.

**Process steps (paragraphs 6 to 10, summarised):**

1. Sponsor (holder of residual risk and/or beneficiary) prepares ECS with operating authorities, acquisition teams, and medical input as needed.
2. Relevant SMEs and the appropriate DSA Regulator review and recommend whether the ECS may proceed (DDSS can help identify the regulator; best practice also consults the statutory regulator, usually HSE).
3. If recommended, sponsor obtains Dir-DSS endorsement and senior manager endorsement (minimum 3*) in the sponsoring organisation, then forwards ECS and draft certificate to SofS or other approving authority. (Named chemical regime exemptions route via QSEE as JSP 418 Leaflet 5 describes.)
4. On success, signed certificate returns to sponsor; sponsor copies Dir-DSS and the DSA Regulator (and DEPR for environmental-protection law); sponsor records the certificate on the appropriate register (for example the Defence Legislation Support Tool).
5. On failure, activity stops until it can comply or a new ECS is approved; sponsor liaises with the DSA Regulator and records the unsuccessful ECS.

**Validity (paragraphs 11 to 12):** assumptions, constraints, and mitigations must remain valid. Significant change (new medical or scientific evidence, surveillance outcomes, occurrences, operating environment changes) triggers formal review and notification to the approving authority when continued validity is in doubt.

## Mental Models

- **Law first, Defence policy second, Area policy third, exemption last.** Expectation 3.5 is not a planning shortcut around 3.1.
- **DED is a defined gap with Defence arrangements, not a blank.** Where statute is disapplied, DDSS policy and DSA regulation are supposed to be at least as good as UK law so far as practicable.
- **Two regulator families.** Statutory regulators enforce statute (with Crown-specific tools against the Department). DSA regulators regulate Defence activity under the DSA Charter, especially where DEDs or Defence-specific regimes apply.
- **Sponsor owns the residual risk story.** The ECS is written by those who would hold residual risk or take the benefit, not by a distant paperwork office alone.

## Anti-patterns

- Writing Area safety policy that quietly conflicts with JSP 815 or other Defence policy (fails 3.2).
- Ignoring legislative change until an inspection finds it (fails 3.1 and 3.4).
- Seeking an exemption before exhausting compliance options, or without ALARP residual controls.
- Running activity under an expired or assumption-broken certificate without review.
- Treating Combat Immunity as a pre-authorised waiver for peacetime or routine risk.
- Pasting statutory regulator or DSA regulation procedure manuals into this pack; cite them by title and read the regulation.

## Key Takeaways

1. Expectations 3.1 to 3.5: maintain compliance mechanisms; keep Area policy consistent; communicate requirements; review on change; manage exemptions/waivers/concessions.
2. JSP 375 is named as Defence's minimum how-to for UK H&S legislation compliance; content stays in JSP 375.
3. DG DSA and eight Defence regulators cover EP, fire, land, maritime, medical, nuclear, OME, and military aviation.
4. Annex B SofS exemption certificates are last-resort, conditioned, usually time-capped, ECS-driven, Dir-DSS and 3* endorsed, regulator-reviewed, and registered.
5. No certificate in force means the activity must conform or stop.
