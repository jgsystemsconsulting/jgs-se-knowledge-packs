## Assurance stages (ch02, ratings in audit findings: ch10)

| Level | Evidence picture (GIAA-aligned) |
|---|---|
| Substantial | Controls fully embedded, managing key risks, operating as intended; no weaknesses identified |
| Moderate | Controls embedded and managing key risks but could be improved; some minor non-compliance |
| Limited | Some but not enough evidence controls operate and are embedded; controls fail in numerous instances |
| Unsatisfactory | Prescribed controls lacking, poorly defined, or not embedded; not measured for compliance or not adhered to |

Rules: the ladder asks three questions of key controls (embedded? managing key risks? operating as intended?); levels are sequential and cumulative, so substantial can only be claimed once moderate is met; Annex H findings use the same levels and also mark non-compliance, improvement opportunities, and good practice.

## Exemption certificate steps (ch04, Annex B)

1. Exhaust every other avenue first. Exemptions are a last resort: SofS must have the legal power, and the case must show national security need or that compliance would directly compromise operational capability.
2. The sponsor (holder of residual risk or beneficiary) builds the ECS with operating authorities, acquisition teams, and medical input as needed, with control measures keeping residual risk ALARP and tolerable.
3. Relevant SMEs and the appropriate DSA Regulator review and recommend whether the ECS may proceed; best practice also consults the statutory regulator, usually HSE.
4. Sponsor obtains Dir-DSS endorsement and senior manager endorsement (minimum 3*), then forwards the ECS and draft certificate to SofS or the other approving authority.
5. On approval, the signed certificate returns to the sponsor, who copies Dir-DSS, the DSA Regulator (and DEPR for environmental law), and records it on the appropriate register (for example the Defence Legislation Support Tool).
6. On failure, the activity stops until it can comply or a new ECS is approved; record the unsuccessful ECS.
7. Keep the certificate valid: conditions hold, assumptions and mitigations stay true, and significant change (new evidence, occurrences, environment) triggers formal review and notification to the approving authority. Certificates are normally time-limited to five years or less.

No certificate in force means the activity must conform or stop.

## Safety case trigger checklist (ch05; equipment cases: ch08)

- Is the activity conducted on, or forming part of, a system (equipment, infrastructure, people, processes, organisational arrangements, or any combination)? If no, ordinary risk assessment and SOPs govern.
- Is a single risk assessment of the activity or workplace insufficient to demonstrate adequate risk control because of complexity, novelty, scale, or the risk profile? Any yes means a safety case is required (E4.7).
- Is the activity simple, non-complex, and routine, held ALARP and tolerable by suitable and sufficient risk assessments plus SOPs or work instructions? Then no safety case: those arrangements remain the primary control.
- The case is a structured argument supported by evidence that the system is safe for a given application in a defined operating environment, demonstrating risks ALARP and tolerable and giving a holistic view of cumulative and interrelated risks.
- Scope is proportionate to risk and complexity, from a single equipment case to a broader case wrapping multiple cases and assessments; the Area may use another label (for example safety management plan) for the same principle.
- Maintain and review the case; equipment safety cases are Element 7's lifecycle application (7.2, ch08).

## Audit cycle steps (ch10, Annex H)

1. Own the purpose: arrangements derive from the SofS Policy Statement and Defence safety policy; JSP 815 is the minimum management arrangements.
2. Set or join an audit programme: DDSS and DSA submit 2LOD/3LOD programmes to the Defence Safety Committee for ratification by end of the preceding December; add audits mid-cycle after Service Inquiries, incidents, or regulator intervention.
3. Plan the audit: the ATL agrees objectives, scope, criteria, method, and timescales and issues formal notification to the organisation head.
4. Hold the pre-audit meeting, normally no later than three months before start: agree scope and outcomes, explain method, arrange escorts, meet safety leads and Trade Union representatives as appropriate.
5. Research and review documents: the standard under test and the auditee's arrangements, including prior audits and close-out.
6. Gather verifiable evidence by sampling, including interfaces; only verifiable information counts as evidence.
7. Open with an in-brief to the Head of Defence Area or empowered representative: scope, method, programme, and debrief format.
8. Evaluate arrangements against policy, legislation, Defence regulation, and performance using interviews, document review, and site or process surveys.
9. Form findings and rate against the GIAA-aligned ladder (Unsatisfactory, Limited, Moderate, Substantial), marking non-compliance, improvement opportunities, and good practice.
10. Verify on site that the rated arrangements are actually in use and effective; record observations.
11. Hold the closing meeting for fact checking with the Area leader, activity owners, and the safety lead.
12. Issue the audit report: narratives per Element heading with observations and recommended corrective actions, conclusions, annexes, and an Executive Summary.
13. Debrief formally, obtain the auditee Action Plan, review its adequacy, and follow up, usually six to nine months later.
14. Improve the programme from lessons, feed outcomes into leadership review (E12.4) and performance reporting (E9), and share good practice.
