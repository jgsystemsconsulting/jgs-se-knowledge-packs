# Application examples: process selection, RIDM, and CRM in practice

## Core Idea

Part 2 exists to illustrate Part 1, not to add doctrine. Its worked examples, all explicitly fictional, sometimes projected into near-future technology, and never to be read as recommended mission designs or as prescriptions for interagency responsibility splits, walk a single thread through the whole framework: an Enterprise-level objective flows down into program/project and institutional objectives, risk processes are selected to fit each context, an Activity-Planning RIDM analysis selects a mission architecture, and CRM executions at a project and a center manage the risks of implementation. The examples deliberately span rigor levels to show the graded approach: some analyses are fully probabilistic with Monte Carlo-derived distributions, others heuristic with anchored risk matrices, and the text shows the two methods can legitimately disagree.

## Frameworks Introduced

- Multi-domain flow-down, made concrete. A primary Enterprise objective, long-term solar system exploration including human missions and surface bases, decomposes into three branches: an institutional objective to build and maintain nuclear space power infrastructure; a programmatic R&D objective spawning nuclear thermal propulsion and nuclear electric power development projects; and an operational objective to execute nuclear-powered missions, exemplified by a planetary surface fission reactor demonstration. Each project in turn generates interface objectives, procurement framework application, test facility readiness, personnel development, transferred to the Institutional Domain. Institutional objectives come in two kinds: agency-wide ones derived from enterprise needs (NEPA and launch approval policy, interagency fuel cooperation with the Department of Energy, a nuclear space systems procurement framework) and project-derived support ones.
- Process selection criteria. RIDM applies at activity set-up, when a risk-informed Analysis of Alternatives helps select mission and system architecture, and during execution, when a risk-control intervention is complex enough to need a formal trade. CRM applies once tasks are defined and underway. Applicability is independent of quantification: where data is missing, a structured qualitative application still yields valuable decision support. The institutional example makes the point; a procurement framework selection trades mostly soft factors (industrial base, cost and schedule advantages, political priorities, administrative flexibility) and is run as an Activity-Planning RIDM with largely qualitative inputs.
- The worked RIDM example, step by step. A hypothetical nuclear surface power delivery mission to a distant planetary mass, sponsored by one directorate with another co-sponsoring the reactor demonstration, gathers stakeholders (science community, safety and mission assurance, environmental groups, mission support offices) and states expectations: a 150 kWe multi-year reactor, a hard 60-month launch window, a $1.2B cost cap, launch safety compliance under National Security Presidential Memorandum-20, a 1-in-1000 planetary contamination goal, workforce development, DoE cooperation, and commercial sourcing where possible. Risk posture is negotiated qualitatively per dimension; medium for technical performance (pioneering demonstration), very low for public nuclear safety, low for cost and schedule, medium for institutional policy preferences; and later translated into quantitative tolerances by iteration, with no prescriptive mapping rule. An objectives hierarchy decomposes project success through technical, safety-and-compliance, and cost-schedule-institutional domains; twelve performance measures are cut to five key ones (mission success probability, radiological release probability, time to launch readiness, project cost, expert staff development ratio). Alternatives combine fuel enrichment, supplier, assembly and launch mode, and vehicle class in a trade matrix (equivalently a trade tree), pruning infeasible and not-recommended combinations to nine feasible alternatives. Influence diagrams, possibly maturing into Bayesian belief networks, map cause-effect dependencies among design choices and measures; Monte Carlo sampling yields CDF or CCDF profiles per alternative, with risk-normalized values read at the declared tolerance levels and constraint violations shown directly.
- The RIDM outcome pattern. Rankings differ per measure; the single heavy-launch alternative leads mission success and schedule; split-delivery alternatives lead radiological safety because subcritical modules eliminate criticality risk; costs converge across vehicle choices; staff development does not discriminate. Given the very low tolerance on radiological release and moderate disadvantages elsewhere, the analysts recommend the alternative that splits the reactor into submodules delivered by two commercial medium-lift launches for later surface assembly, transferring assembly risk to a follow-on mission. Documentation must cover objectives, posture, alternatives, measures, models and assumptions, results, tolerances, and the deliberated rationale.
- The worked CRM examples. A project (the same nuclear delivery mission) and a center (holding project-support duties and an agency-wide cybersecurity core competency) baseline their objectives, measures, and marker/tolerance sets; requirements and goals with distinct RTL-R and RTL-G values, refined from the RIDM-era tolerances as the activity matures. Risk identification draws on OSMA quarterly reporting survey results (aging infrastructure, obsolescence, staff attrition, uncommunicated mandates) plus GAO and OIG findings, and formats findings as standard risk statements. One project risk: reactor power-to-weight below expectation because no reactor of the needed size has ever been demonstrated. Another: plutonium-238 supply constraints limiting flights. A third: combustion instability seen in prototype engine vacuum tests threatening ignition reliability. A fourth: thrust-oscillation coupling with vehicle structural modes. Center risks: test-facility competition from new projects, and facility aging.
- Probabilistic versus heuristic classification, side by side. The engine-instability risk analyzed probabilistically lands Yellow/Marginal (within the requirement tolerance, outside the goal tolerance); the thrust-oscillation risk lands Red (its end-state distribution violates the requirement tolerance, with a payload-adapter-failure branch at certain mission loss). The same engine risk analyzed heuristically lands Red, because expert-judgment conservatisms and the anchored matrix's conservative coloring push marginal cases to red; the handbook states plainly that the two approaches are not guaranteed to agree, and explains why. Aggregation follows: the integrated risk model sequences the scenarios along the baseline path, and the two-scenario aggregate to the loss-of-mission objective reads Yellow; not contradictory, since individual tolerances assume roughly ten contributors, so two scenarios can be individually worrisome while jointly tolerable. The heuristic aggregate of the same scenarios reads Red after the SMEs argue the cumulative end-state likelihood exceeds the goal tolerance.
- U/U risk in practice. Logic models are incomplete by nature; the correction uses the historical ratio of observed to predicted performance (a program predicted at $0.75-1B that cost $3B implies a 3-4x ratio) scaled by leading indicators; complexity, new technology, schedule pressure, management-practice failures.
- Activity-Execution RIDM, two flavors. For the engine risk, the probabilistic version trades three alternatives: accelerated instability prevention funding (fixes the measure to Green but turns schedule and cost Yellow), switching to a mature storable-propellant engine (eliminates the risk but turns cost Red), and no action (engine risk Yellow, everything else nominal). No alternative is green across the board, so the project cannot decide alone; a spider chart shows the second alternative dominated, and the project recommends the first; but the overseeing directorate, holding management reserve, chooses to add funding and rebaseline the schedule goal (kept as an early-warning tripwire) and the cost requirement and goal, illustrating how execution RIDM matures into rebaseline RIDM. For the facility risk, the heuristic version compares refurbish, replace, and modernize against five objectives including long-term test needs and center-of-excellence reputation, with semi-quantitative and qualitative tolerances where the underlying quantities are speculative; refurbish falls short on long-term objectives, replace is dominated, modernize fixes everything but costs unacceptably; leaving two live strategies: accept the narrow fix and spawn new risks for the leftovers, or elevate to the Acquirer for funding, which converts the process into a rebaselining RIDM.

## Mental Models

- Examples are templates, not verdicts: the value is in watching posture drive tolerance values, tolerance values drive classification, and classification drive the response trade.
- When probabilistic and heuristic methods disagree, the disagreement is information: heuristic results embed deliberate conservatism, so a heuristic red on a probabilistic yellow signals thin data, not a mistake.
- An all-green alternative would have let the project decide alone; its absence is what pulls the oversight level into the deliberation: the elevation logic made visible.

## Anti-patterns

The fictional mission concepts are labeled non-prescriptive: they do not suggest actual mission designs, ConOps, or interagency (NASA-DoE) responsibility divisions. Reading the example marker and tolerance numbers as real program data is likewise refused; the tables are stated as hypothetical.

## Key Takeaways

- The same ODRM machinery runs in enterprise, program/project, and institutional settings; only the quantification level changes.
- Qualitative posture statements become quantitative tolerances by iteration, and the risk posture profile (which dimensions are strict) is itself an input to the final recommendation.
- Spider charts comparing response alternatives across affected measures are the standard decision-communication artifact at execution stage.
- Response selection regularly ends in elevation or rebaseline when no alternative is acceptable within existing markers; by design, not failure.
